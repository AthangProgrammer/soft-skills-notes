Week 1: Foundations of Soft Skills and Communication

Lecture 1: Introduction to Soft Skills (full notes)

Soft skills are an umbrella term for people, life and behavioural skills such as self-awareness, flexibility, persistence, friendliness and optimism. They complement hard (technical) skills, make people employable and are learned continuously. Communication is only one part of soft skills. The widening gap between academia and industry, and rising unemployment among qualified people, show why employers value them.

Key facts
  • Soft skills is an umbrella term; they are also called life skills, people skills and behavioural skills.
  • Estimates of the number of soft skills vary (28, 60 or 10) because the skills overlap.
  • Working definition: personality traits, social graces, facility with language, personal habits, friendliness and optimism.
  • Behavioural skills named first: self-awareness, then flexibility, then persistence and perseverance.
  • Soft skills are performance skills and a process of continuous learning.
  • Hard skills are part of the college curriculum; soft skills are not taught as a college subject.
  • I can state the working definition of soft skills.
  • I can list three differences between hard and soft skills.
  • I know why communication skills alone are not soft skills.
  • I can quote Vivekananda’s mantra and explain its three parts.
  • I remember the employability evidence (Census 2011, 23 lakh peon applicants, NASSCOM remark).

Lecture 2: Aspects of Soft Skills (full notes)

Soft skills have several aspects: effective communication (listening, speaking, reading, writing), attitude, adaptability, teamwork, conflict management, leadership, time and space skills, mannerism and cultural sensitivity. Listening is the foundation; business writing aims to express, not impress. Teams, not individuals, are the performance unit, and constructive conflict helps. De Bono’s hats support creative problem solving.

Key facts
  • Latin communicare = to share.
  • 60–75 per cent of one’s time in any job is spent in listening.
  • Four communication skills: listening, speaking, reading, writing; writing is the most challenging.
  • Business writing: express, do not impress.
  • Five negative Cs: criticizing, complaining, comparing, competing, contending.
  • Green hat = generating ideas; white = objective; red = emotion; yellow = positive; blue = control.
  • I can list the aspects of soft skills.
  • I know the four communication skills and why listening comes first.
  • I can match each Six Thinking Hat to its function.
  • I remember Hume, Bidlake and the culture quotation.
  • I can state the 60–75 per cent listening fact.

Lecture 3: Effective Communication Skills (full notes)

Communication is a process in which a sender encodes an idea, sends it through a channel, the receiver decodes it, and feedback completes the cycle. A shared frame of reference, a suitable medium and environment, and a message that is clear, complete, concise, correct, courteous and candid improve effectiveness. Organisational communication may be internal (upward, downward, horizontal, diagonal, including the grapevine) or external.

Key facts
  • Bernard Shaw: the greatest problem in communication is the illusion that it has been accomplished.
  • Communication is a process; feedback completes it.
  • A common frame of reference is essential for success.
  • Conciseness is the hallmark of communication.
  • Grapevine = informal channel, often rumour-based and not always authentic; it may be used carefully.
  • Internal flow: upward, downward, horizontal, vertical, diagonal.
  • I can draw and explain the communication process.
  • I can explain the Shaw quotation.
  • I know the qualities of effective communication.
  • I can distinguish internal, external and grapevine communication.
  • I know the directions of internal communication.

Lecture 4: Classification of Communication (full notes)

Communication may be oral or written and, by participants, intrapersonal, interpersonal, extra-personal, group or mass/media. It is also verbal, non-verbal or meta-communication, carried in print or electronic media, and shaped by culture, ethics and law. Effective communicators avoid plagiarism, libel and slander and communicate ‘to bind, not to divide’.

Key facts
  • Interpersonal communication is also called dyadic communication.
  • Intrapersonal: brain acts as receiver; soliloquy and aside are examples.
  • Mass/media receivers are passive; media include newspapers and television.
  • More than 6 lakh non-verbal cues exist.
  • Meaning lies with the sender, not in words.
  • Written criticism = libel; spoken = slander.
  • I can list the five forms by number of participants.
  • I can compare oral and written communication.
  • I know what meta-communication means.
  • I can distinguish libel, slander and plagiarism.
  • I can give cross-cultural examples from the lecture.

Lecture 5: Personality Development (full notes)

Personality (from persona, mask) is the inner set of qualities, attitudes, values and behaviours that make people distinct. Freud explained it through the id (pleasure), ego (reality) and superego (perfection). Adler, opposing Freud, stressed social interest, fictional finalism and striving for superiority, with the possibility of becoming a creative self.

Key facts
  • Persona = mask.
  • Id: pleasure principle, true psychic reality, matrix of ego and superego.
  • Ego: executive of the personality and organised portion of the id; reality principle.
  • Superego: strives for perfection rather than pleasure; opposes both id and ego; moral guardian.
  • Id relieves tension by reflex action and wish fulfilment.
  • Freud’s book: Interpretation of Dreams.
  • I can explain the origin of the word personality.
  • I can describe id, ego and superego with their principles.
  • I can state Adler’s key ideas.
  • I can contrast Freud and Adler.
  • I remember the quotations and the Thangavelu example.

Week 2: Positivity, Telephone and Non-verbal Communication

Lecture 6: Positive Thinking (full notes)

Positive thinking is the belief that one can succeed; optimists create opportunities while pessimists see obstacles. Eric Berne’s four attitudes show why ‘I’m OK, you’re OK’ suits teamwork. The Johari Window (Luft and Ingham, 1955) uses four areas – open, blind, hidden, unknown – to show how feedback and disclosure increase self-awareness. Positivism is built by believing in one’s abilities, not imitating blindly and pursuing one’s goals.

Key facts
  • Glass half empty = pessimist; half full = optimist.
  • Peale wrote The Power of Positive Thinking.
  • William James: we are only half awake.
  • ‘I’m OK, you’re OK’ = positive and co-operative; ‘I’m OK, you’re not OK’ = imposing; ‘I’m not OK, you’re not OK’ = introvert; ‘I’m not OK, you’re OK’ = negative.
  • Johari Window: Luft and Ingham, 1955; four areas: open, blind, hidden, unknown.
  • Blind area = unknown to self but known by others.
  • I can distinguish optimist and pessimist.
  • I can state all four life positions and their labels.
  • I can define the four Johari areas with known/unknown to self and others.
  • I know who proposed each theory and when.
  • I can list ways to develop positivism.

Lecture 7: Telephonic Communication Skills Part I (full notes)

Telephonic communication bridges time and distance quickly and at low cost but lacks visual feedback, so clarity, courtesy, consideration and brevity are essential. A caller should plan an agenda, place his own call, identify himself, state the purpose and close politely; a receiver should answer within three rings, identify himself, respond positively and take accurate messages. Negative language is avoided.

Key facts
  • Four features: clarity, courtesy, consideration, brevity.
  • Clarity is the first requirement of telephonic communication.
  • Answer a call within three rings.
  • Place your own call and plan an agenda.
  • Identify yourself with name and affiliation when calling and when receiving.
  • Avoid small talk and negative words such as ‘No’ or ‘You have to’.
  • I can list the four features of telephonic communication.
  • I know the steps for making and receiving a call.
  • I can rephrase negative sentences positively.
  • I know the three-ring rule.
  • I can describe how to take messages and transfer calls.

Lecture 8: Telephonic Communication Skills Part II (full notes)

Voice mail replaces short memos, overcomes time zones and cuts paperwork but gives no immediate feedback. Mobile phone etiquette stresses low voice, silence in public places and no odd-hour calls. Teleconferencing connects dispersed people at short notice but is poor for negotiation, while video conferencing adds visuals and has three types; users must manage equipment, speech and body language.

Key facts
  • Voice mail substitutes for short memos and phone calls and solves time-zone difficulties.
  • Voice mail lacks immediate, specific feedback.
  • Teleconferencing is ineffective for negotiations.
  • Teleconferencing can be arranged at short notice and connects dispersed people.
  • Three types of video conferencing (one-way, mixed, two-way).
  • Speak in a low, modulated tone on cell phones; silent mode in meetings and worship places.
  • I can state advantages and limits of voice mail.
  • I know cell phone etiquette.
  • I can contrast teleconferencing and video conferencing.
  • I know the three types of video conferencing.
  • I remember why teleconferencing fails for negotiation.

Lecture 9: Communicating Without Words (full notes)

Non-verbal communication complements words and is spontaneous and unintentional. Birdwhistell put it at 65% and Mehrabian at 55% with 38% vocal. Its categories are kinesics, proxemics, chronemics, paralanguage, haptics and meta-communication. Kinesics covers appearance, face, eyes, movement, posture and gestures, all of which vary by culture and must be controlled to create a favourable impression.

Key facts
  • Birdwhistell: 35% verbal, 65% non-verbal.
  • Mehrabian: 7% verbal, 38% vocal, rest non-verbal.
  • More than 6 lakh non-verbal cues.
  • Non-verbal communication is spontaneous and unintentional.
  • Kinesics = body language; proxemics = space; chronemics = time; haptics = touch.
  • Kinesics forms: appearance, facial expression, eye contact, posture, gesture, movement.
  • I know both percentage splits.
  • I can list the six types of non-verbal communication.
  • I can list the forms of kinesics.
  • I know hand-gesture meanings.
  • I remember the cultural differences in eye contact.

Lecture 10: Paralanguage (full notes)

Paralanguage covers voice quality, volume, rate, pitch, pronunciation and pauses. Speakers should speak at about 140–150 words per minute, vary pitch to avoid monotony, articulate each sound distinctly, follow Received Pronunciation, stress content words and use pauses and silence intentionally. Voice faults such as throatiness, breathiness, nasalisation, harshness, muffling and hoarseness can be reduced by training, except hoarseness, which is medical.

Key facts
  • Ideal rate is about 140–150 words per minute.
  • Pitch changes are called inflections; rises and falls in the voice are intonation.
  • Voice faults: throatiness, breathiness, nasalisation, harshness, muffling, hoarseness.
  • Hoarseness results from inflammation of the larynx; consult a speech therapist.
  • Articles, prepositions, conjunctions, auxiliaries and personal pronouns do not take stress.
  • Mark Twain on the rightly timed pause.
  • I can list the features of voice.
  • I know the voice faults and causes.
  • I remember 140–150 wpm.
  • I know which words take stress and which tones fall or rise.
  • I can quote Mark Twain and Pythagoras.

Week 3: Space, Touch, Meta-communication and Listening

Lecture 11: Proxemics (full notes)

Proxemics studies how space communicates. Four zones – intimate (to 18 in), personal (to 4 ft), social (4–12 ft), public (12–30 ft+) – are linked to relationship and status, and vary with culture. High-context cultures read non-verbal cues while low-context cultures rely on written words. Chronemics treats time as a commodity; punctuality and timing show professionalism, and ‘time talks and space speaks’.

Key facts
  • Four zones: intimate (up to 18 in), personal (18 in–4 ft), social (4–12 ft), public (12–30 ft+).
  • Proxemics derives from proximity.
  • Robert E. Hall: Time talks and space speaks.
  • Low-context: North America, Germany, England, France, Italy, Scandinavia.
  • High-context: Japan, China, Arab countries, Greece, Mexico, Spain.
  • Bosses occupy larger or corner spaces.
  • I know the four zones and distances.
  • I can say who enters each zone.
  • I can classify cultures by context.
  • I can explain chronemics and cite Hall.
  • I remember the Japanese car example.

Lecture 12: Haptics: The Language of Touch (full notes)

Haptics is the language of touch and it varies by culture, gender and setting. Handshakes may express dominance, submission or equality; a dead-fish shake signals disinterest. Gestures with palms and fingers also carry meaning. Because touch can be misread (Thailand’s head taboo, Spanish seven-stroke handshakes), professionals should learn local norms, restrict touch when unsure and ensure verbal and non-verbal messages agree.

Key facts
  • Haptics comes from a Greek word meaning ‘I touch’.
  • Mulk Raj Anand wrote Untouchable; the protagonist is Bakha.
  • In Spain a handshake should last at least seven strokes.
  • In Thailand and Laos touching a child’s head is rude because the head is sacred.
  • Hongi is the New Zealand nose-touching greeting.
  • Three handshake types: dominant, submissive and equal; also dead fish and glove.
  • I know the origin of haptics.
  • I can describe each handshake type.
  • I recall cultural examples (Thailand, Spain, NZ, India, Japan).
  • I know Anand’s Untouchable and Bakha.
  • I can explain verbal–non-verbal conflict.

Lecture 13: Meta-communication (full notes)

Meta-communication is communication beyond words: choice of words, tone, positioning, hesitation and silence. Silence and pauses relieve monotony, give time to think, build rapport and add emphasis, while non-words (ah, um, I mean) harm clarity and show poor preparation. Speakers should provide silence deliberately, keep it short and read others’ silences in context and culture.

Key facts
  • Meta means beyond.
  • Meta-communication may be unintentional.
  • ‘Leave him, not kill him’ example shows the effect of word positioning.
  • Silence can hide meaning or show disagreement.
  • Non-words include ah, um, ‘I mean’, O.K.
  • Adrienne Rich: lying is done with words and also with silence.
  • I can define meta-communication.
  • I can show how word positioning changes meaning.
  • I can list advantages of silence.
  • I know why non-words harm a speech.
  • I can distinguish pause and silence.

Lecture 14: Listening Skills (full notes)

Listening is a conscious mental skill, unlike hearing. It passes through hearing, filtering, interpreting, responding and remembering. Perceptive listening, as defined by Ernest D. Nathan, needs a sincere desire to understand. Listening brings knowledge, relationships, better performance and vocabulary, but prejudging, ambiguity, bias, rigidity and premature evaluation block it.

Key facts
  • Listening process: hearing, filtering, interpreting, responding, remembering.
  • 60–75% of time is spent in listening.
  • Hearing is physiological; listening is mental.
  • Ernest D. Nathan defined perceptive listening.
  • Barriers: prejudging, ambiguity, bias, rigidity, premature evaluation.
  • Listening builds knowledge, relationships and vocabulary.
  • I can distinguish hearing and listening.
  • I can list the five stages in order.
  • I can define perceptive listening.
  • I know the advantages and barriers.
  • I remember 60–75% and SLANT.

Lecture 15: Types of Listening (full notes)

Listening may be combative, attentive or reflective, with reflective listening the most valuable. In organisations content, empathetic, appreciative and analytical listening serve different purposes. Prejudice, noise, ego, preoccupation and language hamper listening, whereas an open mind, concentration, note-taking, patience and attention to non-verbal cues improve it.

Key facts
  • Three basic types: combative, attentive, reflective.
  • Reflective listening is the most important.
  • Types by purpose: content, empathetic, appreciative, analytical.
  • External factors: noise, venue, time, culture.
  • Internal factors: state of mind, ego, language, perception.
  • Do not interrupt, whisper or hold parallel talks in meetings.
  • I know the three basic types.
  • I can match the four purpose-based types to situations.
  • I can list external and internal barriers.
  • I know tips and the Hemingway quote.
  • I understand prejudiced listening.

Week 4: Negotiation, Culture and Organisational Communication

Lecture 16: Negotiation Skills I (full notes)

Negotiation turns conflict into agreement through argument and persuasion. Formal negotiations are pre-announced with an agenda; informal ones are casual. Place, time, atmosphere, personal relations and fear affect results. A negotiator needs confidence, dignity, flexibility, honesty and competence, must prepare facts and limits, secure a BATNA, avoid WATNA and seek a ZOPA that yields a win-win outcome.

Key facts
  • Negotiation arises from conflict and needs two parties.
  • Formal negotiation announces date, venue, time and agenda; three persons involved.
  • Informal negotiation is not pre-announced.
  • Negotiation works by persuasion, not authority.
  • BATNA, WATNA, ZOPA acronyms.
  • Qualities: confidence, appearance, outlook, honesty, competence, flexibility.
  • I can define negotiation.
  • I can compare formal and informal negotiation.
  • I can list factors and qualities.
  • I can expand BATNA, WATNA, ZOPA.
  • I remember Kennedy’s fear quote.

Lecture 17: Negotiation Skills II (full notes)

Negotiation strategy depends on persuasion: style, you-attitude, body language and summarising. It runs through preparation, interaction and implementation. The purple style, blending cooperation and competition, is preferred, supported by collaboration, accommodation, compromise, control and emotional avoidance. Negotiators keep some points in reserve, record agreements, acknowledge cooperation and accept that outcomes can be win-win, win-lose or lose-win.

Key facts
  • Three phases: preparation, interaction, implementation.
  • Red = competitive; blue = cooperative; purple = mix.
  • You-attitude means telling the sunny side.
  • Tim Hindle: almost impossible to over-prepare.
  • Agree and write down the points; decide roles.
  • No negotiation gives a total win to only one party.
  • I know the three phases.
  • I can contrast red, blue and purple.
  • I can list approaches and dos and don’ts.
  • I understand you-attitude.
  • I remember outcome types.

Lecture 18: Culture as Communication (full notes)

Culture is the learnt set of traits, values and behaviours of a group and strongly shapes communication. Globalisation brings cultural contact and, sometimes, cultural shock. Cultures differ in context, individualism, formality, communication style and time orientation; low-context Americans and Germans are direct and word-based, while high-context Arabs, Japanese and Asians stress relationship, silence and non-verbal cues. Respect, deference and learning are the keys to cross-cultural success.

Key facts
  • Culture is learnt and dynamic.
  • GATT and NAFTA promote trade across cultures.
  • Dimensions: context, individualism, formality, communication style, time orientation.
  • Americans: hamburger style; low context; time is money.
  • Mexicans prefer courtesies before business.
  • Arabs consider contracts insulting (high context).
  • I can define culture.
  • I know the five dimensions.
  • I can contrast American, Japanese, Arab and Mexican styles.
  • I can explain cultural shock.
  • I remember translation blunders.

Lecture 19: Communicating across Cultures (full notes)

Communication across cultures requires understanding that culture is a shared code. Low-context cultures trust words and contracts, while high-context cultures read silence, context and relationships. Differences in gestures, numbers, gifts, space, colour and ways of saying no cause misunderstanding. Avoid ethnocentrism and stereotypes; practise tolerance, empathy and adaptation, moving towards ethno-relativism.

Key facts
  • Low context: North America, Scandinavia, France, Germany.
  • High context: Japan, China, Arab countries.
  • Number 8 is lucky; 4 sounds like death in Hong Kong/China.
  • Maneki neko is the Japanese beckoning cat.
  • North Americans keep about 5 feet of space.
  • Sicily raises the chin to say no; Bulgaria nods for no.
  • I can quote Smith’s definition.
  • I can compare low and high context.
  • I know the ways of saying no.
  • I can explain ethnocentrism and ethno-relativism.
  • I remember gift and number examples.

Lecture 20: Organizational Communication (full notes)

Organisational communication carries ideas through formal and informal channels so that goals are achieved. Its functions include forecasting, organising, instructing, coordinating and controlling; managers play interpersonal, informational and decisional roles. Understanding Maslow’s needs and McGregor’s styles helps leaders persuade rather than only control. Statutory and crisis communication, line–staff coordination and a careful use of the grapevine complete the picture.

Key facts
  • Scott’s definition emphasises feedback and action.
  • Functions: forecasting, organising, instructing, coordinating, controlling.
  • Roles: interpersonal, informational, decisional.
  • Maslow’s hierarchy applies to employees.
  • McGregor: traditional versus modern.
  • Flows: vertical, horizontal, crosswise, spiral.

  • I know the functions and roles.
  • I can link Maslow and McGregor to employees.
  • I know the flow types and channels.

Week 5: Communication Breakdown and Business Writing

Lecture 21: Communication Breakdown Part I (full notes)

Communication breakdown occurs when something blocks the flow of a message. Barriers arise at the source (poor word choice, uncertain content, background, inference, perception, rigid thinking, frozen evaluation, allness), between people (vocabulary, emotion, distraction, poor listening, selectivity, noise, imbalance of verbal and non-verbal cues, semantic and cultural gaps) and in organisations. Awareness, feedback and careful choice of words reduce them.

Key facts
  • A. G. Gardiner’s ‘On Saying Please’.
  • Three types: at the source, between people, in the organisation.
  • Homophily means a common frame of reference.
  • Allness uses words like all and every.
  • SOS in a prescription means ‘if required’.
  • Mark Twain’s lightning and lightning bug.
  • I know three types of breakdown.
  • I can list source barriers.
  • I can list interpersonal barriers.
  • I remember Gardiner and Twain.
  • I can give semantic gap examples.

Lecture 22: Communication Breakdown Part II (full notes)

Organisational barriers include tall hierarchies, fear of superiors, negative attitudes, mistrust, subgroups, many transfer stations, wrong media, information overload, jargon, physical distractions and unethical practices such as misquoting. They are overcome by empathy, careful words, active listening, audience-centred messages, an open climate, fewer levels, suitable media and prompt feedback.

Key facts
  • The message sent is not always the message received.
  • Too many transfer stations distort messages.
  • Media richness: choose the channel suited to the receiver.
  • Reduce levels and encourage direct communication.
  • Take immediate feedback.
  • Whitman: what I assume you shall assume.

  • I understand transfer stations.
  • I can list remedies.
  • I remember the passing-away story.
  • I can quote Whitman.

Lecture 23: Advanced Writing Skills (full notes)

Advanced writing is concept-based, researched, drafted, revised and reader-centred. It should be direct, simple and short-sentenced, planned with an outline. Informative writing reports facts; persuasive writing argues. Choose among nine patterns – direct, indirect, order of importance, chronology, problem–solution, causal, spatial, structural, topical – and put bad news in the indirect plan with a positive close.

Key facts
  • Bacon: writing maketh an exact man.
  • Stages: conceptualise, research, draft, revise.
  • Ask why and for whom.
  • Nine patterns: direct, indirect, order of importance, chronology, problem–solution, causal, spatial, structural, topical.
  • Bad news goes in the indirect plan with a positive ending.
  • Short sentences of 6–8 words are simple.

  • I can distinguish informative and persuasive writing.
  • I can list the nine patterns.
  • I can rewrite sentences with the verb first.

Lecture 24: Principles of Business Writing (full notes)

Good business writing follows the 5 Cs. Express rather than impress: use plain, familiar words, cut redundant phrases and avoid foggy sentences. Show courtesy and consideration by taking the reader’s viewpoint, soften negatives, put bad news in the middle and end with hope, and keep cordiality so that relationships and business continue.

Key facts
  • 5 Cs: clarity, courtesy, consideration, conciseness, cordiality.
  • Goal of business writing: to express, not to impress.
  • Eliot: do not use big words; do not say infinitely for very.
  • Put negative news in the middle and end positively.
  • Brevity is the soul of wit.
  • Revision is the last task in writing.

  • I can rewrite rude sentences politely.
  • I remember Eliot and Wilde.

Lecture 25: Types of Business Writing Part I (full notes)

Business writing includes letters, reports, memos, letter reports, circulars, e-mails, proposals and research papers. A memo is a short, informal intra-office document with letterhead, To/From (designation), reference and date, subject line and body but no salutation or close. A letter report is a longer, letter-format report that may go outside; a circular informs larger audiences and has four types.

Week 6: Business Writing and Business Letters

Lecture 26: Types of Business Writing Part II (full notes)

A meeting is documented through a notice (issued days ahead with date, time, venue and agenda), an agenda (numbered items beginning with confirmation of previous minutes and ending with any other matter) and minutes (a signed, past-tense, sequential record). E-mails need brevity, courtesy and care with cc, capitals and attachments. Research papers require a specific title, literature review, outline, statement of purpose, revision and references.

Key facts
  • Notice is pre-information of a meeting.
  • First agenda item: confirmation of previous minutes.
  • Last agenda item: any other matter with the permission of the chair.
  • Minutes are written in the past tense by the secretary.
  • Item numbering such as 5.1 means meeting 5, item 1.
  • Capitals in e-mail are like shouting.
  • I can draft a notice.
  • I know agenda rules.
  • I can write minutes in the right tense.
  • I know e-mail etiquette.
  • I know research paper steps.

Lecture 27: Business Letters (full notes)

Business letters keep organisations and customers in touch, serve as records and reveal the writer’s skill. They are formal, neutral and direct, unlike personal letters. The standard structure has ten parts – letterhead, date, reference, inside address, subject, salutation, body, complimentary close, signature and enclosures – and requires accuracy, courtesy and prompt replies.

Key facts
  • A business letter has at least ten parts.
  • Use ‘Dear Sir/Madam’.
  • Yours faithfully / sincerely; no apostrophe after ‘Yours’.
  • Reference includes ‘our reference’ and ‘your reference’.
  • Body usually has three paragraphs.
  • Identification marks: dictator’s and typist’s initials.
  • I can list the ten parts.
  • I know the date and reference conventions.
  • I know the correct complimentary close.
  • I can explain business vs personal letters.
  • I recall Chesterfield.

Lecture 28: Business Letters: Format & Style (full notes)

Business letters are laid out in four formats – full block (all left), modified block (date, close and signature right of centre), semi-block (indented paragraphs) and simplified (no salutation or close, with subject and attention line). Lecture 39: Report Style: Part II (full notes)

Lecture 57: Facing Job Interviews: Part- I (full notes)

Facing an interview calls for preparation, patience, positivity and nerve control. Let the interviewer lead, listen fully, pause before answering and use tact. Appearance, personality, dress, grooming, voice and mannerisms all communicate; Carnegie stresses that personality (even a lovely smile) can outweigh degrees. Men should wear a well-tailored navy, brown or grey suit, button-down shirt, simple tie ending at the belt centre and polished black shoes; women a conservative business suit or full dress, low-heeled pumps and minimal jewellery. Speak clearly with controlled rate, use pauses, remain polite with tricky questions, and remember that every interview is a learning experience.

Key facts
  • Preparation is the best way to face an interview.
  • Question types: open, closed, probing, reflective, loaded, hypothetical, leading.
  • Let the interviewer lead; do not answer before the question ends.
  • Half the battle is won by nonverbal behaviour.
  • Carnegie’s smile example.
  • Men: navy blue, dark brown or grey suit; black polished shoes.

Lecture 58: Facing Job Interviews: Part II (full notes)

Interviews use seven kinds of questions: open (comfort-building), closed (facts), probing (opinions), reflective (reasoning), loaded (embedded bias), hypothetical (crisis decisions) and leading (suggesting answers). Answers should be logical, honest, affirmative and justified: present weaknesses as merits, avoid disclosing salary or criticising employers, remain modest about the future, show team spirit and ethics, and handle personal questions calmly. Before the interview prepare dress, documents and current awareness; on the day arrive early, talk to other candidates, enter confidently, sit when asked, maintain eye contact and leave gracefully, closing the door gently.

Key facts
  • Seven types: open, closed, probing, reflective, loaded, hypothetical, leading.
  • First question is usually ‘Tell us briefly about yourself’.
  • Closed questions are often followed by justification questions.
  • Hypothetical questions test decision making in crises.
  • Do not disclose salary; do not criticise past employers.
  • Present weakness as a merit.

Lecture 59: Emotional Intelligence and Critical Thinking (full notes)

Emotional intelligence is the capacity to recognise, motivate and manage emotions in oneself and others; it complements IQ, with IQ helping one get a job and EQ helping one succeed and lead. Its personal competencies are self-awareness (emotional awareness, self-assessment, self-confidence), self-regulation (self-control, trustworthiness, conscientiousness, adaptability, innovation) and motivation (achievement drive, initiative, commitment, optimism); its social competencies are empathy (understanding and developing others, service orientation, diversity and political awareness) and social skills (influence, communication, conflict management, leadership, change catalysing, bonds, collaboration, teamwork). Critical thinking adds imagination, relevant questioning, non-judgmental observation, learning and unlearning, awareness of relationships and neutrality. Respect differences: be different, not indifferent.

Key facts
  • EI popularised by Goleman’s Working with Emotional Intelligence.
  • IQ gets the job; EQ ensures success.
  • Five EI components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills.
  • Self-analysis is the first step in EI.
  • Critical thinking improves thinking quality by imposing intellectual standards.
  • Do not be judgmental; ask relevant questions.

  • I can list the five EI components.

Lecture 60: Applied Grammar (full notes)

Applied grammar focuses on common trouble spots: always-plural nouns (police, clothes, scissors, trousers), singular nouns ending in s (news, politics, mathematics, diabetes), capitalisation rules for titles, designations and department names, apostrophes for possession (never in yours), subject–verb agreement (none of, either/or, neither/nor, each of, indefinite pronouns, a great deal of), articles chosen by sound, question tags (including ‘aren’t I?’), prepositions (senior to, since/for, beside/besides, between), voice and transformation, and confusing words (affect/effect, loose/lose, stationary/stationery, later/latter, compliment/complement, desert/dessert, elicit/illicit). The course closes with a message that balanced emotion and intellect, careful communication and constant learning lead to success.

Key facts
  • Police, clothes, scissors, trousers are plural.
  • News, politics, mathematics, diabetes are singular.
  • Children is already plural.
  • Never write your’s or yours’.
  • James’ or James’s both acceptable.
  • Either/or: singular verb for singular subjects; nearest-subject rule otherwise.
