Lecture 41: Leadership Skills

Week 9 · Course book PDF pages 669–683 (printed pages 668–682)

Learning objectives

  • Identify traits and characteristics of a leader.
  • Describe leadership styles: autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, transactional, transformational.
  • Explain initiating structure and consideration, and Fiedler’s contingency model.
  • Describe charismatic and visionary leadership.
  • List cultural differences in leadership and the elements of trust.

Important definitions

Leader

A person from among the group who initiates action, guides members and helps the group reach its goal; not necessarily a manager.

Autocratic leader

Takes decisions on his own ideas, with little interest in members’ problems.

Democratic leader

Considers members’ interests, takes suggestions and arrives at a solution acceptable to all.

Laissez-faire leader

Gives members full autonomy and does not intervene.

Transactional leader

Guides followers towards established goals by clarifying roles and task requirements.

Transformational leader

Inspires followers to transcend self-interest and brings profound change.

Initiating structure

Leader behaviour that defines tasks, work relationships, goals, standards and deadlines.

Consideration

Leader behaviour that builds mutual trust, respects ideas and feelings, and cares for members’ welfare.

LPC (least preferred co-worker)

Scale in Fiedler’s model measuring whether a leader is task-oriented or relationship-oriented.

Charismatic leader

Leader with heroic, extraordinary qualities, vision and commitment, an agent of radical change.

Visionary leadership

Leadership offering a clear, compelling image of an innovative future that taps people’s energy and emotions.

Core concepts

Who is a leader

  • A leader is ‘among us’, not from heaven; example of a passenger who organised a solution after a flight was diverted to Bhopal instead of Jabalpur.
  • Distinguish a manager from a leader.
  • Traits: intelligence, charisma, decisiveness, enthusiasm, strength, bravery, integrity, self-confidence.
  • Characteristics shown: initiative, desire to lead, honesty, integrity, subject knowledge, concern for others.

Styles of leadership

  • Autocratic – not considered good; ignores problems of members.
  • Democratic – involves everyone, weighs pros and cons, acceptable solution.
  • Laissez-faire – autonomy; no interference.
  • Transactional – assigns tasks and roles.
  • Transformational – goes beyond role, sees a new tomorrow.

Dimensions of leader behaviour

  • Initiating structure: assigns tasks, expects standards and deadlines.
  • Consideration: trust, respect, concern, well-being.
  • Leaders high on both bring better products and solutions; too much consideration may cause absenteeism.

Fiedler’s model

  • Fred Fiedler (spelt Fielder in the transcript): effectiveness depends on the match between the leader’s style (measured by LPC) and the degree of control and influence the situation gives.
  • Leadership style is fixed: either task-oriented or relationship-oriented.
  • A relationship-oriented leader may neglect the task and cost the organisation.

Charismatic leadership

  • Followers attribute heroic qualities; leaders show commitment, vision, confidence; agents of radical change (politics, religion, business).
  • Rise above petty self-interest; articulate the vision; participative; pioneers of change; aware of environmental concerns.

Visionary leadership

  • Clear and compelling imagery of an innovative future; draws on traditions and connects to actions people can take.
  • Taps energy and emotions, creating enthusiasm like for sports.
  • Visionary leader explains the vision, has no hidden agenda, expresses it through behaviour and action, extends it to different contexts.

Transactional versus transformational

  • Transactional: roles and task requirements.
  • Transformational: inspires followers to transcend self-interest, instils courage, profound effect.

Cultural differences in leadership

  • Koreans: paternalistic, mentors.
  • Arabs: strict; kindness seen as weakness.
  • Japanese: humble, speak little.
  • Scandinavians and Dutch: do not praise individuals in public.
  • Americans: talk of responsibility, rationality, value orientation and hedonism.

Trust: the hallmark of an effective leader

  • Integrity (honesty, words match action)
  • Competence (subject knowledge)
  • Consistency
  • Loyalty (to organisation and coworkers; represents their case assertively)
  • Openness / candidness

The leader is the sailor guiding the ship; members are passengers.

Closing quotation

Warren E. Bennis: “Leadership is the capacity to translate vision into reality.” Are leaders born or made? Both – they emerge from among us and are made by their qualities (to be seen in group discussion).

Lecture 42: Group Discussion: Part I

Week 9 · Course book PDF pages 684–702 (printed pages 683–701)

Learning objectives

  • Define group discussion (GD) and explain its importance in selection.
  • Differentiate debate and GD.
  • Describe GD set-up: seating, size, time, topic announcement.
  • Differentiate topic GD and case GD.
  • List language, qualities evaluated, individual and group responsibilities, and leadership types in a GD.

Important definitions

Discussion

An exchange of knowledge and experience among people.

Group discussion (GD)

A formal, face-to-face, unstructured, cooperative discussion among participants who exchange views on a topic; a dialogic learning process.

Debate

A formal, structured contest with participants speaking for or against a motion.

Motion

The topic of a debate, supported by the ‘for’ speakers and opposed by the ‘against’ speakers.

Topic GD

A GD on an announced topic, often unstructured and announced on the spot.

Case GD

A GD based on a printed real-life or imaginary case where the group is expected to reach a solution.

Dialogic

In the form of a dialogue among many people.

Bulldozer

A dominating, selfish participant who tries to overpower the group.

Inert participant

A member who does not participate and needs encouragement.

Core concepts

Why GD matters

  • Organisations use GD as a criterion to shortlist/eliminate candidates when applicants are many and vacancies few, often followed by another test.
  • It also helps in crises as a means to find remedies; it has an inbuilt mechanism of correction.
  • Evaluates traits: awareness, reasoning, behaviour, initiative, motivation, assertiveness, listening, leadership, teamwork.

Debate versus GD

  • Both are formal and face-to-face.
  • Debate: structured, fixed sides (for/against), topic given in advance (a week or ten days), competitive, emotional, flowery language.
  • GD: unstructured, flexible (a participant may change stance), topic often announced on the spot, cooperative atmosphere, facts and simple language.

Set-up of a GD

  • Seating circular or semi-circular so all see each other.
  • Usually 7–10 participants.
  • Time: 15, 20 or 25 minutes (decided by organisers).
  • Topic announced; a few minutes to think; keywords ignite thoughts.
  • No rule on who speaks first or in what order; no designated leader.

Topic GD versus case GD

  • Topic GD: clear topic announced.
  • Case GD: detailed printed case (real or imaginary); tests analytical, decisive and reasoning skills; aim is a solution; suggestions of ways may be offered.
  • Flexibility is the hallmark of both.

Language and phrases

  • Simple, concise, appropriate, persuasive and polite; avoid flowery, pompous, obsolete or sesquipedalian words.
  • Starting: “Today we have gathered to discuss…”.
  • Strong view: “I strongly feel…”, “I am convinced…”, “I am pretty sure…”.
  • Neutral: “According to me…”.
  • Making a point: “May I make a point about…?”, “I think it is important to consider…”.
  • Agreeing: “I fully agree with what Mohan says.”
  • Disagreeing politely: “That may be your personal view; my view is…”.
  • Returning to the topic: “Could we stick to the topic?”, “I fear we are deviating…”.

Qualities evaluated

  • Awareness, reasoning with facts, behaviour (group dynamics matter more than content), initiative, motivation (encourage the quiet), assertiveness (not aggression), listening, leadership and team skills.

Responsibilities

  • Individual: speak, behave well, listen, show interest.
  • Group: commit to group goals, keep a conducive and amicable atmosphere, resolve conflicts, ensure full participation, keep cohesion; differences should be ideological, not personal.

Leadership in GD

  • No designated leader; anyone can lead at any time; tested in conflict or chaos.
  • Driver – initiates.
  • Amiable – speaks little, neutral; not acceptable.
  • Democrat – best; restores order and shares time.
  • Bulldozer – dominating, selfish; can be cut to size by exposing weak logic.

Body language

Must never be negative; accept opposition graciously through eyes, face and posture; show commitment. “A group swims and sinks together.”

Summary

Group discussion is a formal, unstructured, cooperative, dialogic exchange used to shortlist candidates. Unlike a structured, competitive debate with fixed sides, GD allows flexible stances and uses simple, factual, polite language. Held in a circle or semi-circle with 7–10 participants for 15–25 minutes, it may be a topic GD or case GD. Lecture 43: Group Discussion: Part II

Week 9 · Course book PDF pages 703–718 (printed pages 702–717)

Learning objectives

  • Explain the role of an emergent leader and the LEADER acronym used in the lecture.
  • Describe what panelists observe in a GD.
  • List the dos for preparing and participating in a GD.
  • List the don’ts in a GD.
  • Recognise types of GD topics and the need for decorum.

Important definitions

Emergent leader

A leader who is not designated but emerges from the group by taking initiative to bring the discussion back to the main issue.

Panelist

The evaluator who observes participants’ speech, behaviour and non-verbal conduct, usually noting points.

Inert (silent) participant

A member who does not participate; he becomes a liability to the group.

High / low participator

Members who speak often or rarely; panelists note changes in participation.

Chairman role

Giving judgment; participants must not assume it.

Bulldozer

A dominating member who creates chaos; can be countered by exposing flaws in his logic.

Decorum

Proper behaviour in a discussion, free from gender, cultural or ego bias.

Core concepts

Role of leader in GD

  • Saves the group from being sidetracked (like a boat in the middle of a river).
  • Resolves conflicts, summarises, encourages silent members, keeps participation going.
  • Leader types: specific, implied, emergent, designated – in GD the leader is emergent.
  • Influence by constructive participation and rational thoughts; build support among supporters and logically weaken opponents’ view.

LEADER acronym (lecture’s analysis)

  • Liberal – not rigid; ensures everyone participates.
  • Eager and enthusiastic.
  • Assertive – without arrogance; influence by constructive ideas.
  • Democrat – everyone gets a chance; nobody’s sentiment is hurt.
  • Erudite – subject knowledge.
  • Rational – no discrimination.

Also: good communication ability. (The lecture lists these traits in running speech; the letter mapping follows the speaker’s explanation.)

What panelists observe

  • Who started, who speaks more or less, who became silent, who keeps the ball rolling.
  • Reactions to others: gesture, posture, pitch and loudness of voice.
  • Whether anyone is hurt (religion, ethics) in controversial topics.
  • Shifts in participation: support, opposition or attempts to subdue.
  • Consistent involvement – not one speech followed by ‘golden silence’.

How to prepare / dos

  1. Seat yourself comfortably (non-verbal behaviour).
  2. Listen to the topic carefully; keywords ignite thoughts.
  3. Organise thoughts before speaking; speak complete, grammatical sentences with follow-up points.
  4. Speak at the earliest opportunity without snatching; be patient.
  5. Politely intervene if someone speaks too long: “May I share my piece of mind…”.
  6. Identify supporters and opponents; augment or counter.
  7. Keep track of time; share time fairly.
  8. Maintain eye contact while speaking and listening.
  9. Persuade silent members politely.
  10. Use a proper pitch, intonation and articulation; speak at a pace others follow.
  11. Define the problem when sidetracked; look at both sides of the coin.

Don’ts in a GD

  • Do not be in a hurry to start; never remain silent.
  • Do not dominate; influence through constructive, rational, factual views.
  • Never take the role of a chairman or an extreme, judgmental stance.
  • Do not argue with opponents or whisper with supporters; no value judgments.
  • Do not look at the panelist to impress; do not use slang to shout at silent members.
  • Do not move excessively forward or show physical weight.
  • Do not put all ideas in one go or speak too fast.
  • Do not digress; avoid gender, cultural or ego issues.

GD topics

  • Factual (e.g. brain drain should be stopped; human cloning should be banned), abstract, fictional, controversial (e.g. no reservation in jobs; transfer of religious places; ‘money makes happy, but too much money makes us unhappy’).
  • Take a solid stand based on logic; discuss, do not quarrel; consensus is desirable but not always possible.

Lecture 44: Meeting Management

Week 9 · Course book PDF pages 719–731 (printed pages 718–730)

Learning objectives

  • Define a meeting and list its functions.
  • Identify reasons why meetings fail.
  • Plan a meeting by answering why, who, when, where and what.
  • Conduct a meeting: start on time, follow the agenda, allow participation, record minutes.
  • State participant etiquette and the leader’s closing duties.

Important definitions

Meeting

An organised gathering of people for a specific purpose, called to share information, train, solve problems, plan, receive/demonstrate products and make decisions.

Agenda

A list of items to be discussed, circulated in advance to give members first-hand information.

Minutes

The official record of what was discussed and decided; unpleasant incidents are not recorded.

Venue

The place where the meeting is held.

Fiasco

A meaningless or failed meeting with no outcome.

Follow-up action

Assigning responsibilities and deadlines after the meeting.

Core concepts

Functions of a meeting

  1. Information sharing in the unit.
  2. Training of new members and for new practices.
  3. Problem solving and crisis management, using diverse resources of the group.
  4. Planning and taking stock of work in progress.
  5. Receiving / demonstrating a new product (e.g. e-rickshaw) to find lapses.
  6. Decision making – the overall function of every meeting.

Why meetings fail

  • Poor planning – notice of 2–3 hours, no agenda or unprepared members.
  • Poor leadership.
  • Too many routine meetings; half-hearted, disinterested participation.
  • Meetings at very short notice (except emergencies).

Questions before calling a meeting

  • Why – is a meeting really necessary or is a discussion enough?
  • Who – only people whose presence matters.
  • When – check calendars and convenience of key people (by e-mail); avoid evenings after 7 pm; first half preferred by some.
  • Where – accessible common place; change venues for a series but keep cost low.
  • What – agenda circulated beforehand.

Arrangements

  • Check the venue well in advance: capacity (6–7 to 20 people), electricity, coolers or AC, gadgets, microphones for large groups.
  • Pad and pen at each seat; refreshments if possible.
  • Agenda well prepared and systematic.

Conducting the meeting

  • Start on time even if some are late (do not scold latecomers; may say ‘we started without you’).
  • Follow the agenda; keep the discussion from deviating.
  • Allow full participation; democratic control; encourage quiet members politely (“Well, Mr X, what is your opinion?”).
  • Appoint a secretary to record minutes; use official, positive language; do not record unpleasant incidents.
  • Be task-centred; finish on time.

Etiquette for participants

  • Wait for your turn to speak.
  • Switch off cell phones; no satirical language.
  • If the meeting overruns because of a domineering person, ask politely: “May I know if the meeting is over?”
  • Be an active participant, not a mute spectator.

Closing the meeting

  • Summarise the outcomes; identify specifics.
  • Assign responsibilities and set deadlines; decide follow-up.
  • Thank the leader/participants; announce that minutes will be circulated.
  • Leader resolves conflict neutrally in polite, persuasive language.

Lecture 45: Adaptability and Work Ethics

Week 9 · Course book PDF pages 732–749 (printed pages 731–748)

Learning objectives

  • Define adaptability and explain why employees must adapt.
  • Define ethics and work ethics.
  • Explain mutual obligations of employer and employee, including Kant’s view that employees are ends, not merely means.
  • List employee rights: freedom/non-discrimination, privacy, due process, participation, health and safety, fair wages, speech.
  • Identify unethical practices and ways to be ethical.

Important definitions

Adaptability

The ability of an individual or organisation to change itself or its responses to new circumstances, situations or environments; shows the ability to learn.

Ethics

A sense of righteousness that tells whether actions are right or wrong.

Work ethic

A set of values based on the moral virtues of hard work and diligence, belief in the moral benefit of work and its ability to enhance character (as quoted from Wikipedia).

Code of conduct

Written rules and regulations of an organisation; supported by unwritten personal codes.

Moral hazard

Risk that parties in an employer–employee relationship behave unethically.

Plagiarism

Reproducing another person’s words or ideas as one’s own.

Pilfering

Stealing small things slowly and steadily from the organisation.

Misquoting / misrepresenting data

Reporting what was not said or distorting data to gain advantage.

Probation period

Initial period to judge an employee’s capability.

Core concepts

Why adaptability

  • Organisations change practices: paperless offices, electronic pay slips, online tax returns.
  • Resisting change makes people rigid, isolated and a barrier to communication; adaptable people learn and improve themselves and the organisation’s fitness.
  • Change is the law of nature; follow the organisational flow of communication.
  • Computer literacy spread because people adapted.

Skills needed to adapt

  • Physical fitness (a sound mind in a sound body).
  • Mental and psychological preparedness.
  • Willingness to work in a multicultural setting.
  • Learn the organisation’s culture and code of conduct; remember that you are its ambassador.

Personal ethics at work

  • Commitment, reliability, loyalty to organisation over friends and relatives.
  • Keep confidential information secret (finance, banking passwords).
  • Regular attendance for pay received.
  • Honouring the appointment letter, contract and probation; leaving for a small perk soon after joining is an ethical issue.

Employer–employee relationship

  • Based on trust and legal obligations; rights come with duties.
  • Organisation provides pay, facilities, environment and self-actualisation.
  • The employer has authority to monitor and control, and may invest in the employee.
  • Immanuel Kant: employees should be treated as ends, not only means; occupation gives awareness of one’s powers and happiness; ‘man feels contented after heavy work’.

Employee rights

  • Right to freedom / non-discrimination – on gender, colour, faith, age, disability.
  • Right to privacy – personal data and habits, except where safety demands (e.g. testing a pilot for alcohol); work–life balance (no odd-hour duties for women).
  • Right to due process – equal criteria for promotion and fair treatment in layoffs with adequate time and compensation.
  • Right to participation and association – consulted in decisions such as closing a unit.
  • Right to health and safe working conditions – well-being measures, helmets, yoga, swimming.
  • Right to fair wages – same pay for same work.
  • Right to freedom of conscience and speech – but not anti-organisational.

Unethical practices to avoid

  • Plagiarism.
  • Misusing office resources (phone, property); pilfering.
  • Selective misquoting.
  • Taking credit for others’ work.
  • Misrepresenting data; distorting visuals; exaggeration.
  • Criticising the organisation or colleagues publicly.
  • Conflict of loyalties: personal ethics versus organisational code (e.g. evaluating a loss-making unit that will cause redundancies).

How to be ethical

  • Follow written and unwritten codes of conduct.
  • Be honest, transparent, sincere, hardworking, an ambassador of the organisation.
  • Love your work; speak well of the organisation.
  • Protect confidential information.
  • Respect all faiths and beliefs in a multicultural workplace.
  • Balance rights and duties.
