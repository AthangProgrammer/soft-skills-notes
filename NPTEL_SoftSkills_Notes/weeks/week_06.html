Lecture 26: Types of Business Writing Part II

Week 6 · Course book PDF pages 411–428 (printed pages 410–427)

Learning objectives

  • Draft a notice, agenda and minutes of a meeting with correct format and language.
  • Write professional e-mails following netiquette.
  • Differentiate research paper types: journal paper, conference paper, review article, thesis, term paper, technical paper.
  • Plan a research paper: title, literature review, outline, statement of purpose, revision, references.

Important definitions

Notice

Pre-information about a meeting stating the issuer, venue, date, time and agenda; sent several days in advance.

Agenda

From agendum: the list of items to be discussed in a meeting, prepared with the chairman; usually attached to the notice.

Minutes

A sequential written record of the happenings in a meeting, prepared by the secretary in consultation with the chairman, in the past tense.

Note of dissent

A written disagreement a member may submit when he does not agree with the minutes or decisions.

Research paper

A document presenting a new finding or analysis, supported by evidence and references.

Review article

An article that examines and evaluates a new book, film or development in a field.

Core concepts

Notice of a meeting

  • Organisation name at top centre; heading NOTICE.
  • State the number of the meeting (e.g. 10th meeting), body (board of directors / executive committee), date, day, time, venue and mention that the agenda is attached.
  • Issue well in advance (4–5 days) unless urgent.
  • Signed by the secretary (hard copy).

Agenda

  • Items numbered as 5.1, 5.2 (meeting number.item number).
  • First item: confirmation of the minutes of the previous meeting.
  • Last item: any other matter with the permission of the chair.
  • Prepared with the chairman; not attached for urgent or secret meetings.

Minutes

  • Written by the secretary after the meeting (a day or two later), finalised with the chairman and circulated.
  • Heading: organisation, Minutes of the Nth meeting, date and venue; chairman first, then members present; absentees listed after the secretary.
  • Language: simple, specific, past tense, often passive (“was discussed”).
  • Items follow the agenda sequence: 10.1 confirmation of minutes, 10.2 chairman’s report, 10.3 provision of laptops for office staff, … any other matter (canteen) ... “The meeting ended with a vote of thanks to the chair.”
  • Secretary signs, chairman signs on the left, with date.
  • Not everything is recorded (off-the-record remarks); minutes serve as a record for future reference.
  • An e-mail is only a medium; minutes are authentic when signed.

E-mail etiquette

  • Use proper salutation for official mail; keep it short and specific; no sarcasm.
  • Avoid capital letters (they are like shouting); use correct punctuation.
  • Limit cc to those concerned (information load).
  • Keep the signature line brief; check that the attachment is really attached.
  • Avoid long emotional e-mails; remember that e-mails can be official records.

Research papers

  • Organisations use R&D and incubation centres.
  • Types: technical paper, conference paper (presented to peers), journal paper (for publication), review article, thesis, term paper.
  • Steps: select a specific title → literature review in libraries → outline → statement of purpose → analysis and findings → conclusion → revision (language, grammar, addition, deletion) → bibliography/references.
  • Title capitalisation: main words only, not prepositions or connectives.
  • Examples: “Importance of soft skills at workplace”; “Role of media in women empowerment”.

Lecture 27: Business Letters

Week 6 · Course book PDF pages 429–449 (printed pages 428–448)

Learning objectives

  • Explain the functions of business letters and how they differ from personal letters.
  • List the ten parts of a business letter and the rules for each.
  • State the correct salutation, closing and signature conventions.
  • Recall Lord Chesterfield’s advice on politeness.

Important definitions

Business letter

A formal written communication between an organisation and a customer, firm or individual for business purposes: direct, neutral in tone, simple in language, kept as record.

Letterhead

The printed heading with organisation name, logo, address, telephone, fax and website.

Inside address

The address of the recipient, written accurately with title and full name.

Subject line

A clear one-line statement of what the letter is about.

Complimentary close

The polite closing phrase such as ‘Yours faithfully’ or ‘Yours sincerely’.

Identification marks

Initials of the dictator and the typist, e.g. BM/LKI.

Enclosures

Attached documents; the number of pages is stated in brackets.

Core concepts

Why business letters still matter

  • Maintain rapport with customers, clients and shareholders; speed up business.
  • Serve as record for future reference and quasi-MOU (memorandum of understanding) of the dealings.
  • Promote products, get feedback, expand business beyond a region.
  • The writer represents the organisation; reveals personality and ability; employers want written skills.
  • Horace Walpole's lady: “I write to you because I have nothing to do; I finish my letter because I have nothing to say.”

Business versus personal letters

  • Tone: business letters are neutral, formal, direct, cordial; no sarcasm or emotion.
  • Content: only business; personal letters (Charles Lamb, Thomas Gray, Carlyle, Robert Lynd, A. G. Gardiner, Lord Chesterfield) can talk about weather and family.
  • Language: simple; no sesquipedalian (long) words or long sentences.
  • Time is a commodity; come to the point at once.

Ten parts of a business letter

  1. Letterhead – follow your organisation’s style (centre, left or right).
  2. Date line – month first (March 10, 2017) or numeric; follow organisation practice.
  3. Reference – both ‘Our ref.’ and ‘Your ref.’.
  4. Inside address – full, accurate; use Shri/Dr/Mr/Ms; if name unknown write designation.
  5. Subject line
  6. Salutation – ‘Dear Sir/Madam’ (do not drop ‘Dear’); comma optional in full block.
  7. Body – usually 3 paragraphs: opening (direct), discussion/details, closing.
  8. Complimentary close – Yours faithfully/sincerely (no apostrophe in ‘Yours’).
  9. Signature line – typed name in brackets; leave two lines gap for signature; ‘for’ when writing on behalf.
  10. Enclosures (and identification marks) – state number of pages; e.g. cheque, receipt, order copy.

Closing lines

  • Avoid participial endings such as “Waiting to see you” or “Hoping to get your response”.
  • Use complete sentences: “I look forward to receiving your answer soon.”, “I hope to hear from you soon.”
  • Yours truly / yours cordially are less common; a student may write ‘yours obediently’. Yours faithfully (unknown name) or yours sincerely are safest.

Politeness

Lord Chesterfield: politeness is as much concerned in answering letters within a reasonable time as in returning a bow immediately. Letters are an exchange of goodwill and build relationships.

Lecture 28: Business Letters: Format & Style

Week 6 · Course book PDF pages 450–466 (printed pages 449–465)

Learning objectives

  • Describe the four letter formats: full block, modified block, semi-block (indented), simplified.
  • State the punctuation rules (open punctuation) and placement of date, close and signature for each.
  • Apply style rules: reader-friendly, you-approach, thank-you opening, positive language, active/passive voice, I versus we.
  • Rewrite negative letter sentences positively.

Important definitions

Full block format

All parts (date, address, salutation, subject, body, close, signature, enclosures) are aligned to the left margin; open punctuation; the most common format in organisations.

Modified block format

Address, subject, salutation and body paragraphs on the left; date, complimentary close and signature between the centre and the right margin.

Semi-block (indented) format

Like modified block but paragraphs are indented.

Simplified format

Used when the recipient’s name or gender is unknown: no salutation, no complimentary close; subject line and attention line; date and writer's name aligned on the right.

Open punctuation

No punctuation in the inside address, salutation and complimentary close; punctuation only in the body.

Attention line

A line (in capitals) directing the letter to a particular person or post, e.g. ATTENTION: Finance Officer.

Core concepts

Four formats

  1. Full block – everything on the left; no commas after date or address; double space between salutation and subject; typed name in brackets with gap for signature; designation below.
  2. Modified block – date, close and signature between right margin and centre; body blocked; sometimes a colon after ‘Dear Sir’.
  3. Semi-block – same as modified, but paragraphs indented; date in the middle or right; inside address at left.
  4. Simplified – no salutation or close; use subject and attention line; writer's name on right; like a memo; easy to type.

Always follow the practice of your organisation.

Salutation when gender is unknown

Write ‘Dear’ plus the first name (Dear Rahil, Dear Stella, Dear Stephanie).

Opening and closing

  • Reply letters begin with thanks (“Thank you for your letter dated …” / mention reference no.).
  • Come straight to the point: “Please find enclosed…”.
  • Last line should be cordial, reinforcing goodwill (you-approach); mention enclosures.

Style of business letters

  • Reader-friendly, you-approach – imagine receiving your own letter.
  • Courtesy – thank-you note connects with earlier correspondence.
  • Consideration – short, to the point; do not write in anger; do not blame the reader; avoid many negatives.
  • Be specific – mention model, colour, design in orders; specific letters get quicker replies.
  • Simple and precise words, natural tone.
  • I versus we: use ‘we’ when representing the organisation; ‘I’ for personal informal tone.
  • Voice: active for good news; passive for indirect or defensive messages.

Positive rewriting

  • “If you do not pay on time, we will lose you as a customer” → “We appreciate timely payment to continue a relationship with our customers.”
  • “Your refrigerator’s warranty has expired; service on payment” → “We welcome you as a valued customer; for goods out of warranty, service is available on payment.”
  • “We are glad we can now send your Xerox machine” → “Please find your new Xerox machine; we look forward to receiving orders from our valued customers.”
  • “Your application cannot be entertained; try next year” → “May we request you to submit a detailed resume with full educational details?”
  • “We are not responsible for damages in transportation” → “Though we take care in quality control, things can go wrong in transit; we are always ready to serve you.”

Lecture 29: Types of Business Letters Part I

Week 6 · Course book PDF pages 467–485 (printed pages 466–484)

Learning objectives

  • List the types of business letters.
  • Write a sales letter using the AIDA technique.
  • Draft letters of enquiry, responses to enquiries, order letters, credit letters and refusal letters.
  • Apply Sir Ernest Gowers’ advice on courtesy.

Important definitions

Sales letter

A letter written like an advertisement to promote a product and convert the reader into a customer.

AIDA

Attention, Interest, Desire, Action: the pattern for sales letters.

Letter of enquiry

A letter seeking information about a product or service; main idea first, polite command ending in a period.

Order letter

A letter placing an order; specific, direct, lists items vertically and states the payment procedure.

Credit letter

A request to purchase on credit, supported by valid credit references.

Refusal letter

A letter declining a request without breaking goodwill; gives reasons and keeps hope.

Core concepts

Types of business letters

Sales letters, letters of enquiry, placing orders, letters of credit, collection letters, refusal letters, claim letters, adjustment letters and letters of recognition. All business letters are goodwill letters.

Sir Ernest Gowers’ rule

  • If he is rude, be specially courteous.
  • If he is muddle-headed, be specially lucid.
  • If he is pig-headed, be patient.
  • If he is helpful, be appreciative.
  • If he convicts you of a mistake, acknowledge it (with gratitude).

Planning any letter

Three As: Analysis, Anticipation, Adaptation – determine your purpose, anticipate the reader's reaction, adapt and organise carefully; proofread.

Sales letter and AIDA

  • Introduction (Attention): striking statement, significant point, quotation, question, special appeal, central selling point.
  • Body (Interest and Desire): trial offers, guarantees, free samples, brochures.
  • Close (Action): positive call to action, e.g. “Send your order today and be the first in town to own our new television.”
  • Avoid exaggeration (ethical limit) and negatives (“Do not miss…”).

Letter of enquiry and its reply

  • Enquiry: main idea first; polite command ending in a period; appreciation at the end (“I appreciate this information, which will enable me to decide on 45 ACs.”); subject line should be clear.
  • Reply: good news first; avoid dull openings (“Lying before me is your letter…”); begin “Please find the information you requested…”; answer all queries sequentially; use lists or bullets; enclose brochures/coupons to promote other products.
  • Mixed messages: give good news first, place bad news before the end, close positively (e.g. accept the talk but propose the last week of March because of a conference in Sweden).

Order, credit and refusal letters

  • Order letter: first line “Please send me 45 Hitachi window ACs”; be specific; list items vertically; mention payment procedure (cheque, banking); cordial conclusion.
  • Credit letter: give sufficient valid credit references and offer additional information.
  • Refusal of credit: thank the applicant; explain (company is new); welcome cash purchases; promise future credit arrangements; keep the customer.

Lecture 30: Types of Business Letters Part II

Week 6 · Course book PDF pages 486–503 (printed pages 485–502)

Learning objectives

  • Write a collection letter series with a gradually firmer tone.
  • Draft a claim (complaint) letter: state the problem, explain, state the adjustment wanted, close cordially.
  • Draft an adjustment letter: good news first, active voice, explanation, empathy, appreciation.
  • Write a recommendation letter honestly and quote the closing remark on simplicity.

Important definitions

Collection letter

A letter, or series of letters, written to recover money owed; tone moves from gentle to firm.

Claim / complaint letter

A letter written to correct a wrong and obtain replacement, repair or compensation.

Adjustment letter

The organisation’s reply to a claim; conveys the remedy while restoring goodwill.

Recommendation letter

A letter nominating and justifying a person for promotion, awards or recognition, written honestly and confidentially.

Empathetic language

Language that places oneself in the customer’s position (“You are right to be disappointed…”).

Core concepts

Collection letters

  • A series (often four) because one letter may be ignored.
  • 1st: gentle reminder; 2nd: a bit harsh; 3rd: harsher and indirect; final: strong, may be intimidating but still ends cordially.
  • Sample final letter: “Despite three reminders we have not received the outstanding amount for 20 photocopiers… You are morally obliged to pay… further delay may force us to hand the matter to legal agencies… Please send the cheque and inform us by telephone by 5 p.m. tomorrow.” (deadline set)
  • Credit and collection go hand in hand; keep the customer if possible.

Claim letters

  • Receiving complaints is good: an opportunity for dialogue, relationship and product improvement and feedback.
  • Open with the problem or the action wanted (“Please send your service engineer to rectify the technical problems in 20 geysers…”).
  • Explain what went wrong without alleging; appeal to the company’s fair reputation (“we have always enjoyed your prompt service”).
  • State the inconvenience and the adjustment wanted (replacement or refund).
  • Do not blame or shout; include copies of bills, receipts and documents; suggest remedy.
  • Close cordially: “We realise mistakes in ordering and shipping sometimes occur; because we have enjoyed your prompt service, we hope you will send the ACs by …”.
  • Example: two employees with confirmed reservations were refused rooms – “would you clarify your room reservation policy for late arrivals?”

Adjustment letters

  • Complaints are more valuable than surveys.
  • Open with the good news (action taken) in active voice: “We have sent the new photocopier to your address” – not “has been sent”.
  • Explain why things went wrong; give additional information; emphasise the action wanted from the customer.
  • Address the claimant by name.
  • Avoid negative words (trouble, fault, regret, misunderstanding); do not blame the customer or staff; avoid unrealistic promises (“this will never happen again”).
  • Use sensitive, empathetic language: “We are really sorry; you are right to be disappointed with the Lumia cell phone” (Gowers: if he convicts you of a mistake, admit it).
  • Close with appreciation: “Thanks for writing. Your satisfaction is important to us… our goal is to earn your confidence.”
  • Send appreciation notes when a wrong is righted.

Recommendation letters

  • Purpose: nominate and justify the merits for promotion, awards or recognition.
  • Be honest, confidential, not biased; do not exaggerate; avoid vague or ambiguous words.
  • Mention if you are writing at someone's suggestion.
  • Evaluate communication, organisational, leadership, team and behavioural skills and dynamism.
  • Do not include negatives without evidence; keep a copy for your record.
  • Closing quotation about letters: “The art of art, the glory of expression and the sunshine of the light of letters is simplicity” (attributed to a famous poet; the transcript gives no name).

Summary

Collection letters grow firmer across a series but keep goodwill. Claim letters state the problem and desired remedy politely with documents; adjustment letters open with the good news in active voice, explain, show empathy, avoid blame and negative words and thank the customer. Recommendation letters must be honest, specific and confidential. Simplicity and cordiality underlie all letters.
