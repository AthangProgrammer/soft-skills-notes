Lecture 51: Making Effective Presentations

Week 11 · Course book PDF pages 832–851 (printed pages 831–850)

Learning objectives

  • List the features of an effective presentation: beginning, voice, body language, humour, examples, ending.
  • Explain the role of practice and rehearsal, including timing and self-recording.
  • Describe delivery factors: expressiveness, rate, volume, pitch, articulation, appearance.
  • Use visual aids, PPT slides and connectives effectively.
  • State tips for the delivery day and recall Freud and Confucius.

Important definitions

Effective presentation

A presentation that captivates at the start, is clear in voice and body language, uses humour and familiar examples, integrates aids and ends emphatically.

Rehearsal (practice)

Repeated, timed practice of the presentation, ideally recorded and reviewed.

Expressiveness

The quality of voice and body in conveying the intended meaning.

Rate

Speed of speaking in words per minute.

Pitch movement / intonation

Rise and fall of voice to give emphasis and emotion.

Connectives (signposts)

Transitions, internal previews and internal summaries that show the audience where the speaker is.

Lectern

A stand for notes that may also hide nervousness.

Desensitisation

Telling oneself positive things and visualising success to reduce anxiety.

Core concepts

Effective beginning

  • Capture attention: a question, story, anecdote, episode or startling fact; avoid an abrupt start.
  • Example: “There is one thing common to all of us…and that is nervousness.”
  • Memorise the first lines and any quotation fully, with the name of the author, to avoid embarrassment.

Voice and body language

  • Voice: clear and articulate; speak slowly enough that words are not eaten; voice can be trained.
  • Body language: eye contact with all sections of the audience; stand firmly; use space naturally; gestures that match the message; avoid too frequent movement and fidgeting.
  • Freud: “He who has eyes to see and ears to listen can convince himself that no mortal can keep a secret…betrayal oozes out of him at every pore.”

Humour and examples

  • Humour relieves technical subjects (e.g. dentist joke) and sustains attention.
  • Use familiar examples suited to the audience (sports, music, movies; for yoga, a mix).

Ending

End emphatically with a restatement and signposts (‘before I close…’); summarise main points; do not end abruptly or limply.

Practice and rehearsal

  • Confidence cannot be gained in a day; rehearse repeatedly.
  • Prepare a final outline; do not read word for word.
  • Time yourself with a clock; cut points if too long; record on a cell phone to review body language, eye contact and voice.
  • Be your own critic; give small presentations to friends for honest feedback; use a mirror.

PPT slides

  • Use slides as an outline: only essential points, a limited number per slide; no clutter or reading from slides (same as reading from manuscript).
  • Animation without ‘music of thought’ is useless.

Delivery factors

  • Expressiveness of voice and body.
  • Rate: moderate – the lecture cites 120–130 words per minute versus 180 as too fast.
  • Volume: audible; adjust to hall, crowd and acoustics; keep a little distance from the microphone.
  • Pitch and intonation: vary emphasis.
  • Articulation: clear syllables.
  • Appearance: formal, presentable dress, haircut, bearing; avoid distracting items (earrings, cell phones).

Visual aids and connectives

  • Integrate aids at the relevant point; interpret them; do not stand between the aid and the audience (OHP failure).
  • Use pointers properly.
  • In speech we use connectives instead of punctuation: transitions, internal previews, internal summaries; signposts such as ‘finally’, ‘last but not the least’, ‘before I close’.

Tips

  • Prepare thoroughly, practise repeatedly, time yourself.
  • Use a lectern if needed; microphone etiquette (no shouting).
  • Stress busters: desensitise, visualise success, begin with a pause, speak the first sentence from memory, keep eye contact, slow down.
  • Move naturally; ignore smiles and whispers or treat them as appreciation.
  • Do not read, do not memorise everything, no long introduction, no ‘verbal bullets’, avoid frowning.
  • Keep writing and speaking when using a blackboard.
  • Stand with one foot ahead of the other; avoid fidgeting.
  • Give handouts after; stay for questions; if you cannot answer, offer to discuss later; end with summary and appreciation.
  • Confucius: “A superior man is modest in his speech but exceeds in his actions.”

Summary

An effective presentation opens with a captivating hook, uses a clear voice at a moderate rate, appropriate volume and varied pitch, supportive body language and eye contact, humour and familiar examples, well-integrated aids and signposts, and an emphatic ending. Success depends on rehearsal: outline, timing, recording and feedback. Slides should be an outline, not a script. On the day, begin with a pause, stay natural, dress formally, handle questions gracefully and end with a summary. Lecture 52: Speeches for Various Occasions

Week 11 · Course book PDF pages 852–868 (printed pages 851–867)

Learning objectives

  • Explain why professionals need to give occasion speeches.
  • Differentiate speeches from presentations.
  • Stress the importance of the beginning and closing, with the Antony and Frost illustrations.
  • Describe the types: welcome, introductory, felicitation, commemorative, farewell, vote of thanks.
  • State the do’s of each type.

Important definitions

Speech

A formal, subjective, emotion-soaked oral address for a specific occasion, without visual aids, depending on voice and language.

Welcome speech

A speech welcoming the chief guest and the audience, with accurate facts about the guest.

Introductory speech

A speech introducing a guest or speaker to the audience, praising achievements without embarrassment or exaggeration.

Felicitation speech

A speech honouring and celebrating a person’s achievements or award.

Commemorative speech

A speech honouring the memory of a person who is no more, in a sorrowful tone.

Farewell speech

A leave-taking speech, in a subdued tone, by the organisation or by the departing person.

Vote of thanks

Closing speech thanking everyone in order of seniority with reasons, briefly and warmly.

Core concepts

Why speeches

  • We maintain social and professional relationships; occasions arise at work: welcoming guests, retirements, honours, tributes, farewells.
  • Notice may be only a day or two, so always be ready.

Speech versus presentation

  • Presentation: technical, complex, objective, with visual aids.
  • Speech: emotional, sentimental, subjective, no visual aids; the speaker is the visual.
  • Language: may be ornate or figurative but must stay simple and understandable; style varies by occasion.

Power of opening and closing

  • The beginning should generate intimacy; the closing leaves a lasting impression (Longfellow: great is the art of beginning, but greater the art of ending).
  • Opening illustration: in Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar Antony wins over a hostile crowd by a courteous, subtle opening that asks for their attention and gradually reverses their opinion of Caesar as a traitor; ‘half the battle is won in the beginning’.
  • Closing illustration: a convocation address ending with a famous poem’s lines about promises to keep and miles to go (Robert Frost) reminds graduates that life is a greater examination.

Welcome speech

  • Gather facts about the guest; get the name and achievements exactly right.
  • Welcome the chair, dignitaries, the guest and the audience; say why the guest is important and how the audience will benefit.
  • Example: welcoming a renowned Indian English poet-novelist to a conference of English teachers.

Introductory speech

  • Start with salutations and an engaging note; give achievements in short sentences.
  • Do not speak too long; no embarrassing or untrue claims.
  • Example: introducing Mukesh Ambani – joined Reliance in 1981, chairman in 2002, awards for telecom; end by inviting applause.

Felicitation speech

  • Honour achievements; talk about the laurel, award or recognition and its impact.
  • Example: felicitating a nominee to the World Bank for work benefiting farmers; use auspicious, respectful language.

Commemorative speech

  • Honour the memory of a departed person; talk of loss and contribution.
  • Tone is sorrowful; words figurative and suited to the occasion.
  • Example: Pandit Nehru’s address on the death of Mahatma Gandhi – light gone out, ‘Bapu’, ‘Father of the Nation’.

Farewell speech

  • Subdued tone; talk of the person’s association and contribution; mention that he will be missed.
  • If you are the one leaving: control emotions, thank organisers, be polite, brief and humble; do not exaggerate your contribution.
  • No criticism and no excessive praise.

Vote of thanks

  • Acknowledge everyone, in order of seniority, with reasons; include supporting staff.
  • Brief but warm; change the language from the report acknowledgement; a touch of humour is welcome.
  • Example: ‘I am thankful to Mr X for his valuable contribution…’.

Closing quotation

Buddha: the wise fashion speech with thought, sifting it as grain is sifted through a sieve – speak only filtered, valuable thoughts. Work hopefully and prepare for every speech situation.

Summary

Professionals must be ready to give speeches on occasions. Unlike presentations, speeches are emotional, subjective and without visual aids, so language, style, and especially the opening and closing matter, as illustrated by Antony’s speech and a convocation ending. The six types are welcome, introductory, felicitation, commemorative, farewell and vote of thanks, each with its own tone and conventions: accurate facts, respect, no exaggeration, humility, brevity and warmth. Speak only sifted, valuable thoughts.

Lecture 53: Interviews

Week 11 · Course book PDF pages 869–884 (printed pages 868–883)

Learning objectives

  • Define an interview and explain the origin of the word.
  • List the types of interview: informative, persuasive, exit, evaluation, counselling, conflict resolution, disciplinary, termination, job.
  • State general skills needed by an interviewee.
  • Describe the stages of the recruitment process.
  • List attributes recruiters look for.

Important definitions

Interview

A face-to-face, mostly oral, systematic communication in which an interviewer tests the traits and qualities of an interviewee for a purpose; from French ‘entrevue’ (a sight between), glossed in the lecture as ‘to see the other person’.

Interviewer / interviewee

The person taking the interview / the person being interviewed.

Information-gathering interview

An interview to collect data, e.g. by phone or Skype when time is short.

Persuasive interview

An interview to convince a person, e.g. to close a deal or to correct performance.

Exit interview

An interview with a departing employee to learn problems and get valuable suggestions.

Evaluation interview

A periodic progress review between a senior and a subordinate.

Counselling interview

An interview to motivate and boost the morale of employees.

Conflict-resolution interview

An interview to resolve conflict between departments or persons.

Disciplinary interview

A fact-finding interview by a disciplinary committee after a mistake.

Termination interview

An interview before ending an employee’s service (may involve a ‘golden handshake’).

Stress interview

An interview that tests the candidate in a stressful situation with challenging questions.

Screening

First stage where applications and CVs are sifted.

Core concepts

Nature of an interview

  • Occurs in jobs, admissions, hospitals, hostels and organisations throughout life.
  • Orality combined with non-verbal skills.
  • Pauline V. Young: an interview is a systematic method by which a person enters imaginatively into the inner life of a comparative stranger.
  • Nervousness arises because interviewers are strangers.

Types of interviews

  1. Informative (data collection; telephonic, Skype).
  2. Persuasive (convincing in deals or to improve performance).
  3. Exit – leaving employees give honest, constructive inputs; avoid bad-mouthing.
  4. Evaluation – progress reviews.
  5. Counselling – morale boosting.
  6. Conflict resolution between departments.
  7. Disciplinary – fact finding.
  8. Termination – final discussion; golden handshake.
  9. Job interview (selection).

Skills for the interviewee

  • Confidence – built by understanding the purpose and gathering information.
  • Listen carefully to the whole question before answering.
  • Anticipate questions; use intelligence for tricky ones (like Yorkers, googlies and bouncers in cricket).
  • Use formal language; no informal expressions.
  • Know your strengths and weaknesses; present weaknesses so they become merits; avoid over- or underestimating yourself.
  • Understand the interviewer’s needs and expectations.
  • Behave well: every person present is observing.

Importance of preparation

John D. Singleton: “Very often the difference between the person hired and the person rejected is not who is the better candidate, but who is better prepared for the interview.” Years of learning are judged in half an hour; careers can be made or lost there.

Stages of recruitment

  1. Screening of applications and CVs (saleable résumé is essential).
  2. Competency tests to eliminate candidates.
  3. Psychological tests.
  4. Group discussion / case.
  5. Negotiation.
  6. Stress interview.
  7. Medical test (last).

What recruiters look for

  • A good person, competent, an asset to the organisation; well informed and well rounded; good personality.
  • Attitude, adaptability, aptitude, behaviour, team spirit and dynamics.
  • Clarity of purpose, thought and expression; decision-making ability.
  • Originality and innovative mind; cheerful disposition.
  • Find information through newspapers (e.g. Times of India Ascent), Employment News and company websites.

Getting started

Mark Twain: the secret of getting ahead is getting started. Build confidence, revise the lectures on personality and positive thinking, prepare a good résumé to get the call (next lecture).

Lecture 54: Planning and Preparation: Part-1

Week 11 · Course book PDF pages 885–902 (printed pages 884–901)

Learning objectives

  • List the preparatory steps before applying for a job.
  • Explain self-analysis: who am I, achievements, strengths, weaknesses, values, personality, background, hobbies and career goals.
  • Explain why researching the organisation and the job position matters.
  • Differentiate intuitive skills from learned skills.
  • Describe the interview file and the will to win.

Important definitions

Self-analysis

Honest examination of one’s qualifications, achievements, strengths, weaknesses, values, personality and goals before choosing a job.

USP (unique selling product/proposition)

The one skill in which a person is most confident, which becomes a personal trademark for which they are called.

Intuitive skills

Skills already present within a person, such as adaptability, assertiveness, creativity, resourcefulness and discretion, which can be further developed.

Learned skills

Skills acquired through effort, such as computer programming, marketing, interpersonal skills, speaking, organising events, time management, coordination, leadership and writing.

Interview file

An organised file of résumé, certificates, degrees, experience letters, awards, scholarships and newspaper clippings prepared before the interview.

Corporate culture

The work culture, language, values and ways of functioning of an organisation.

Panel interview

An interview in which several people judge the candidate together.

Will to win

The desire to succeed and the urge to reach one’s full potential (Confucius, as cited).

Core concepts

Preparatory steps

  1. Analyse yourself (self-analysis).
  2. Identify your skills.
  3. Research the organisation.
  4. Know the job position (roles, responsibilities, expectations).
  5. Revise subject knowledge and update skills.
  6. Draft the résumé and build the interview file.

Choice alone is not the only ingredient in getting a job; qualification, skills and fit matter. Choosing a career in line with one’s qualification avoids becoming a misfit.

Know yourself: who am I?

  • People either underestimate or overestimate themselves.
  • Ask ‘Who am I?’: think of qualifications, achievements (debates, speeches, sports, best paper award) and strengths.
  • Everyone has some quality: speaking, writing, convincing, leadership, initiative, computing (C++, Oracle).
  • Match strengths to jobs: good talkers and interviewers suit HR or placement cells.

Planning, goals and values

  • Have a plan: e.g. write books, become CEO, head the training and placement cell, general manager or regional head.
  • Be clear about goals; review past achievements (papers, patents, books, contribution to the field).
  • Prioritise the skill in which you are most confident; it becomes your USP.
  • Decide what you value: money, status, power, position, recognition as a researcher, administrative acumen.
  • Values shape the job choice: administrative service for those wanting a group; a laboratory or cabin for researchers.

Personality and behaviour

  • Do you work with a group or in isolation? A person who wants to work in isolation is not fit for today’s organisations.
  • Mental alertness, anticipation and foresight (a visionary).
  • Work style: aggressive or democratic; slow but steady; perfectionist or specialist.
  • How fast or slow you think.

Background, hobbies and goals

  • Background: schools, exposure to the outside world, accomplishments (grades, distinctions, papers).
  • Hobbies and interests may be asked, since recruiters want a well-rounded person, not merely bookish; workplace requires social relationships and responsibility.
  • Career goals must be defined.

Research the organisation

  • Visit the company website (e.g. Wipro, Infosys, L&T, Reliance, Reliance Jio): operations, growth rate, history, work culture, language of correspondence, new products.
  • Know the corporate culture, faiths and cultures of an MNC workforce and the language used.
  • Questions may come from the organisation’s recent achievements; be updated.

Know the job

  • Understand roles, responsibilities, challenges, special duties, career prospects, enhancement opportunities and promotions.
  • You may be asked which task you can do more comfortably; psychologists may create challenging situations to test your response.
  • Anticipate beforehand.

Update subject knowledge and skills

  • Revise subject knowledge; a master’s done long ago needs updating.
  • A skilled person never sits idle (e.g. a cobbler, car or bike mechanic).
  • Today’s organisations need multiple skills; a person who says he will do only one job is a ‘gone case’.

Intuitive and learned skills

  • Intuitive (inborn): adaptability, assertiveness, creativity, resourcefulness, efficiency, leadership, broad-mindedness, discretion, ability to assimilate differing opinions.
  • Learned: computer/programming skills, marketing and deal making, interpersonal skills, speaking on occasions, organising events/conferences/workshops, training, time management, coordination, administrative and leadership skills, writing for different occasions.

Interview file and will to win

  • Develop the interview file well in advance; do not be at ‘sixes and sevens’ on the day.
  • Contents: résumé that sells, certificates, degrees, diplomas in proper order, experience certificates, conference/competition/workshop certificates, awards, scholarships, fellowships, newspaper clippings for a quick glance.
  • Be confident that the recruiter is waiting for you.
  • Confucius (as cited): “The will to win, the desire to succeed, the urge to reach your full potential: these are the keys that will unlock the door to personal excellence.”
  • Winners do things with a difference; those not selected get another chance.

Learning objectives

  • Differentiate résumé and CV with their etymologies.
  • State the purpose and requirements of a standard résumé.
  • List special skills and personal attributes recruiters want.
  • Describe the three formats: chronological, functional, hybrid.
  • Explain the parts of a résumé opening: heading, career objective, summary of qualification.
  • State do’s and don’ts of résumé writing.

Important definitions

Résumé

From French, ‘to summarise’: a document summarising one’s achievements, education, experience and future intentions to create interest in one’s candidature.

Curriculum vitae (CV)

From Latin, ‘the course of life’ / details of qualification: a more detailed document than a résumé; the two terms are often used interchangeably.

Career objective

A specific statement of the position sought and the skills to be used, expressing short- and long-term goals.

Chronological résumé

Lists experience in time order from the latest to the earliest; suits experienced candidates.

Functional (skill) résumé

Emphasises skills and abilities; suits entry-level candidates.

Hybrid / combinational résumé

A mix of chronological and functional formats.

Action verbs

Past-tense verbs that ‘speak’, such as accomplished, assisted, demonstrated, identified, investigated, participated, supervised, streamlined.

References (referees)

People who can verify one’s capability; supplied if asked.

Scannable résumé

A résumé on good-quality paper that can be scanned or sent as an attachment.

Core concepts

Résumé versus CV

  • Résumé (French): a summary; CV (Latin): curriculum vitae, more detailed.
  • Used interchangeably; some organisations ask for one, some for the other.
  • A résumé accompanies an application letter.

Purpose of a résumé

  • The first round of selection (elimination) is through the résumé.
  • Its purpose is to kindle interest in your candidature and win an interview call.
  • Many candidates fail by being too weighty or poorly framed.

Requirements of a standard résumé

  • Formatted (no handwritten résumé today) in a systematic manner; many organisations give a proforma, others test your own format.
  • Specific to the advertised post and its required skills.
  • Error-free in grammar and spelling.
  • Broken into units, with crisp phrases and specific words; use action verbs.
  • Positive image: ‘sell’ yourself (e.g. academically gifted, leader); relate your best features and relegate weaknesses; do not overestimate yourself.

Contents

  • Career goals/objective, education, work experience, activities, honours, rewards, awards, special skills, references.

Special skills

  • Computer proficiency (paperless office).
  • Foreign-language proficiency (multicultural workplaces).
  • Writing ability and interpersonal/human skills.
  • Problem-solving and decision-making.

Personal attributes

  • Enthusiasm, initiative, motivation, adaptability, sense of humour, friendliness; also gregarious, self-starter, manager, good speaker/writer, creative, communicative.
  • Today’s organisations are people organisations.

Education, experience and references

  • Education: school, college, university, other courses.
  • Experience: even brief or small experiences count; freshers can cite fests, group projects, reports, responsibilities, with their role clearly stated.
  • Mention gaps and job changes accurately; longer service helps; be honest if terminated (they may ask).
  • References: former or present employers, colleagues, professors; not relatives; write ‘available on request’ and give only when asked.

Types of résumé

  1. Chronological – experienced; latest to earliest; shows work style.
  2. Functional / skill – entry-level; highlights skills.
  3. Hybrid / combinational – blend of the two.

Opening of a résumé

  • Heading: exact name as on certificates (no nickname), permanent and campus address, an active phone number, a frequently checked email, and URL/web page if any.
  • Career objective: specific, not generalised. Examples: ‘To obtain a position as a software engineer allowing me to utilise my knowledge of Oracle and Linux’; ‘To obtain a post of marketing support representative where skills in administration and management, program development, public relations and writing can be used’.
  • Summary of qualification: start with the highest qualification, in order (e.g. B.Tech in computer science with specialisation in networking).

Style and length

  • Use active statements and phrases consistently, not a mix of sentences and phrases; begin with action verbs; avoid weak words like ‘help’; avoid ‘I was in charge of…’.
  • Length: 1 to 1.5 pages for entry-level candidates; even one page is fine if it shows skills.
  • Use good-quality paper; the résumé may be scanned.

Do’s and don’ts

  • Do: mention major activities, job objective, use proper headings, be honest, be specific, update regularly.
  • Don’t: write an autobiography; list every strength (keep something for the interview); mention salary; criticise a previous employer; overestimate yourself.
  • Statements in the résumé must match reality.
