Lecture 1: Introduction to Soft Skills

Week 1 · Course book PDF pages 5–23 (printed pages 4–22)

Learning objectives

  • Understand what soft skills are and why the term is called an umbrella term.
  • Distinguish soft skills from hard skills.
  • Explain why soft skills matter for employability and career growth.
  • Recall the working definition and the key skills named in the lecture (self-awareness, flexibility, persistence).

Important definitions

Soft skills

An umbrella term for people skills, life skills and behavioural skills. Working definition: soft skills comprise personality traits, social graces, facility with language, personal habits, friendliness and optimism that mark people to varying degrees.

Hard skills

Subject knowledge and technical expertise gained through college or university study; part of the formal curriculum.

Employability

The ability to get and keep a job; the lecturer calls it the “keyword”, since degrees alone do not make a person employable.

Core concepts

Soft skills are more than communication skills

Many people think soft skills = communication skills. Communication is only one part. Others include interpersonal skills, management skills, leadership skills and team dynamics. Soft skills cannot be learned in a day: they are a lifelong, continuous learning process.

The lecturer’s two illustrations

  • Gift-shop manager: a young manager with no degree won the customer by helping, analysing the need, being friendly and reassuring. Another salesman who also spoke did not have the “extra edge”, so communication alone is not the answer.
  • Two doctors on one road: both had foreign degrees and were toppers, yet one clinic was crowded. The difference lay in how they talked to patients, their use of simple language (the doctor who said “hypochondria” confused the patient) and how friendly the staff were.

Behavioural skills the lecture names

  • Self-awareness – awareness of present-day needs of the world and of one’s own abilities.
  • Flexibility.
  • Persistence and perseverance.
  • Self-confidence, optimism, cultural sensitivity, being accommodating and a good listener.

Why soft skills matter

  • They help us come out of difficult situations and give reassurance and reliability.
  • Two equally qualified people can get different packages because of soft skills.
  • Job requirements are changing: employers want people who take initiative, make decisions, negotiate and innovate.
  • There is a gap between academia and industry; organisations often train new recruits in soft skills.
  • Soft skills make an employee more cordial, accountable and able to take decisions without the boss’s direction.
  • Needed in meetings, group discussions, drafting business documents, telephone deals, electronic writing and representing the organisation.
  • In a globalised “global village”, cultural sensitivity and listening are essential.

Evidence cited (employability gap)

  • Census 2011 data reported by The Hindu: unemployment is high among the better qualified.
  • Report: 23 lakh people applied for a peon’s job in U.P., including engineers and doctors.
  • Survey in Aspiring Minds: many professionals are unemployable because of poor communication skills.
  • Kiran Karnik (former NASSCOM president): we must groom qualified students to make them employable.
  • ILO report: India faces “jobless growth”; Rituparna Chakraborty (Vice President, Indian Staffing Federation): “We need to invest in and build skills among the youth.”
  • Government initiatives mentioned: Skill India, Start-up India, Make in India; NPTEL itself aims to bridge the gap.

Lecture 2: Aspects of Soft Skills

Week 1 · Course book PDF pages 24–40 (printed pages 23–39)

Learning objectives

  • List the main aspects (components) of soft skills: communication, attitude, adaptability, teamwork, conflict management, leadership, time and space skills, mannerism and cultural sensitivity.
  • Explain the four communication skills (listening, speaking, reading, writing) and why listening comes first.
  • Apply De Bono’s Six Thinking Hats to problem solving.
  • Recall the quotations and thinkers used in the lecture.

Important definitions

Effective communication

Communication that serves the requisite purpose (e.g. you order a book and receive it in time). It needs more than words: structure, sentence construction, non-verbal cues and the receiver’s point of view.

Communication

From the Latin communicare, meaning to share an idea or experience through a medium.

Attitude

A tendency or willingness displayed to get work done in the right direction and in the right way.

Adaptability

Ability to adjust to differing situations, tools, versions and ways of working.

Homophily

Common experiences or familiarity between sender and receiver, which make communication faster and more vital.

Conflict management

Using soft skills to resolve a conflict so that it does not turn into a quarrel; healthy conflict is functional and promotes creative thinking and team decisions.

Core concepts

Aspects of soft skills

  1. Effective communication – listening, speaking, reading, writing.
  2. Attitude.
  3. Adaptability.
  4. Teamwork and conflict management.
  5. Leadership – a trait developed over time; leaders emerge from critical situations (e.g. rescue a group discussion from being sidetracked).
  6. Time and space skills – everyone has only 24 hours; effective managers manage time.
  7. Mannerism – learnable; organisations look for well-mannered people.
  8. Cultural sensitivity – respect others’ culture, faith and beliefs.
  9. Problem solving (e.g. Six Thinking Hats).

The four communication skills

  • Listening – first and most neglected; 60–75 per cent of working time is spent listening. All hearing is not listening: hearing needs no conscious effort, listening is a mental activity (active/perceptive listening).
  • Speaking – needed in interviews, group discussions, data collection, product launches, negotiations, presentations. A communication starts with a message and ends in a response.
  • Reading – on the decline because of screens; “Reading maketh a full man”.
  • Writing – the most challenging; business writing is meant to express and not to impress; avoid pompous words; follow the organisation’s structure.

Attitude: the five Cs to avoid

Organisations suffer from “emotional cancers”: criticizing, complaining, comparing, competing and contending instead of concentrating on one’s own work. (“My shirt is whiter than yours.”)

Teamwork and conflict

  • Projects come as team projects; a leader should bind, cohere and accommodate different natures.
  • Own both defeat and victory: no blame game; say “team’s victory”.
  • Suzanne Bidlake: “In most models of future organization, teams, not individuals function as the primary performance unit.”
  • David Hume: “Truth springs from argument amongst friends” – let there be argument, not quarrel. Emotions should not enter conflict resolution.
  • Advantages of conflict management: functional, healthy, ensures creative thinking and promotes team decisions.

Culture

The lecture quotes “Culture is a code we learn and share” (the transcript says Adam Smith; the quiz attributes the definition to Alfred G. Smith, Communication and Culture). Accept, accommodate and assimilate; communication should be a binding, not a dividing, force. Not everything in your culture is good, nor everything in another’s bad.

Leadership

Not all can be leaders. A leader emerges because of merit and constructive suggestions, builds support logically, and saves the group from deviating. Leadership is developed through awareness and interaction.

Six Thinking Hats — Edward De Bono

A technique for problem solving: when all members take turns wearing different coloured hats, different kinds of ideas emerge, giving more creative and conclusive solutions.

Quotations to remember
  • David Hume – “Truth springs from argument amongst friends.”
  • Suzanne Bidlake – teams, not individuals, are the primary performance unit.
  • “Reading maketh a full man.”
  • Culture is “a code we learn and share” (Alfred G. Smith).

Examples and applications

Example

Missed calls used by youngsters as a code: repeated missed calls lead to confusion, showing communication can fail.

Example

Cricket: the captain says “it is the team’s victory”, not the individual’s.

Example

In a group discussion a person with no points at first can contribute later if he keeps listening patiently.

Lecture 3: Effective Communication Skills

Week 1 · Course book PDF pages 41–54 (printed pages 40–53)

Learning objectives

  • Define communication and explain why it is called a process.
  • Describe the elements of the communication process: sender, encoding, message, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback, frame of reference, noise.
  • State the qualities of effective communication (the 7 Cs as presented).
  • Differentiate general-purpose and organisational communication, and internal and external communication, including the grapevine.

Important definitions

Communication

To express an idea, desire, expectation or experience; from Latin communicare (to share). It is effective only when it meets its desired goal.

Common frame of reference

Shared background (age, experience, knowledge, tastes) between sender and receiver that makes communication successful.

Encoding

The sender’s act of putting an idea into a message (words, symbols, language) before sending.

Decoding

The receiver’s act of extracting the meaning from the received message.

Feedback

The receiver’s response; only feedback shows that the communication process is complete.

Grapevine

An informal channel of organisational communication, often a rumour mill; information may be unauthentic or inadequate, though it may also forewarn management.

Homophily

Common experiences between communicators.

Core concepts

Illusion of communication

George Bernard Shaw: “The greatest problem in communication is the illusion that it has been accomplished.” The real test is whether the message sent has been received properly.

Communication as a process

  1. The sender/source has an idea, experience, desire or fact.
  2. The sender considers the receiver’s background, encodes the idea in a message and selects a channel.
  3. The message is transmitted; obstruction (noise, wrong medium, difficult language) may change it.
  4. The receiver decodes the message.
  5. The receiver gives feedback, which completes the process.

Absence of a response does not always mean failure; there may be an obstruction.

Factors that affect success

  • Perception differs between sender and receiver.
  • Environment (e.g. a noisy marketplace spoils a phone call).
  • Medium (the teacher who did not have email access); choose a medium the receiver is accustomed to.
  • Language and vocabulary (Marathi to a Tamil speaker fails; difficult English words fail because nobody sits with a thesaurus).
  • Receiver’s attention and background.

Qualities of effective communication (the 7 Cs as presented)

  • Clarity – be clear and avoid difficult words.
  • Completeness – no half sentences; written messages cannot be clarified on the spot.
  • Conciseness – use short sentences yet be specific; the “hallmark of communication”.
  • Correctness – grammatical and accurate.
  • Courtesy – consideration and sympathy for the receiver, especially seniors and elders.
  • Candidness – integrity, honesty and fairness.
  • Consideration of the receiver’s background and knowledge (the text names the seven but spells out these six plus consideration; the exact seventh appears only in the original slide).

Organisational communication

Differs from casual communication; words must be specific because people of different faiths, beliefs and positions work together. Think of collective responsibility.

  • Internal – within the organisation: formal (meetings, policies) and informal. Flow: upward, downward, horizontal (lateral), vertical, diagonal/across departments.
  • External – with clients, customers, government agencies and the public; builds a positive image (promotion, website, pamphlets, letters, internet).

The grapevine

Formal communication is to the point, so employees seek an informal channel. Grapevine information is often unauthentic and can create factionalism; managers sometimes use it carefully for early warnings. Use it not to discard or discredit, and stay away from rumour.

Lecture 4: Classification of Communication

Week 1 · Course book PDF pages 55–69 (printed pages 54–68)

Learning objectives

  • Compare oral and written communication.
  • Classify communication by number of participants: intrapersonal, interpersonal, extra-personal, group and mass/media.
  • Explain verbal, non-verbal and meta-communication, and print vs electronic media.
  • Understand cross-cultural, ethical and legal aspects (plagiarism, libel, slander).

Important definitions

Intrapersonal communication

Communication within oneself: you talk to yourself; the eyes act as transmitter, the central nervous system as medium and the brain as receiver, with feedback in bodily reactions. Helps self-discovery and recovery.

Interpersonal communication

Communication between two individuals (or two groups), usually conversation or dialogue; also called dyadic communication and ‘relationship communication’.

Extra-personal communication

Communication between humans and animals (or animals among themselves) without language; responses come through movements and sounds.

Group communication

Communication among members of a group (classroom lecture, group discussion, meeting).

Mass and media communication

Communication to large audiences through electrical and mechanical devices (newspaper, television); receivers are largely passive.

Meta-communication

Communication beyond words, including unintentional meanings that listeners find in what is said.

Libel and slander

Criticism of an organisation or person in writing is libel; in speech it is slander. Both are unethical and may be illegal.

Plagiarism

Using someone’s words or ideas without acknowledgment; to be avoided with misquoting.

Core concepts

Oral and written communication

  • Oral: spontaneous, warm, immediate feedback and clarification, uses voice to carry emotion, can be informal or formal, reaches small or large groups; forms include briefing, talk, speech, group discussion, paper presentation, meetings, interviews and telephone.
  • Written: organised, formal, a record; feedback is delayed; follows standards of grammar and structure; cannot be easily changed once sent; can reach a very large audience across the world. Business writing includes letters, reports, memos, notices, newsletters, circulars, brochures and research papers.

Four forms by number of participants

Humans communicate in four (five) ways depending on the number of participants: intrapersonal, interpersonal, extra-personal, group and mass/media.

  • Intrapersonal examples: soliloquy and aside in plays (Hamlet’s “To be or not to be”).
  • Interpersonal depends on the relationship with the other party (e.g. boss). Interviews are interpersonal.
  • Group: silence in a meeting also communicates.
  • Mass/media: less chance of clarification and less personal rapport; sender cannot control a wide audience.

Verbal, non-verbal and meta-communication

  • All spoken or written communication is verbal (uses words). Meaning lies with the sender, not the word.
  • Non-verbal cues supplement words (body movements, face); the lecture says there are more than 6 lakh non-verbal cues. “Whatever you say is written on your face.”
  • Meta-communication is beyond words and may carry unintended meaning.

Print and electronic media

Written words keep their importance in print and electronic form (email, chat). Think before writing: messages can be interpreted differently and may have impact in a crisis.

Cross-cultural communication

  • Respect religious sentiments, etiquette, tastes, habits and the concept of time.
  • In Latin America meetings may start late because people first discuss and eat.
  • Gifts: giving a Chinese person a clock may suggest inviting evil.
  • Do not assume your culture’s understanding is universal; allow room to receivers; mind status patterns.

Ethical and legal aspects

  • Every organisation has an ethical code and policy statements; employees must respect them (e.g. using organisational funds to travel for a personal interview is unethical).
  • Avoid misquoting, plagiarism and inaccurate information; acknowledge sources.
  • Do not criticise your organisation to get another job; remember you are its ambassador.

Communication may be oral or written and, by participants, intrapersonal, interpersonal, extra-personal, group or mass/media. Lecture 5: Personality Development

Week 1 · Course book PDF pages 70–83 (printed pages 69–82)

Learning objectives

  • Explain the origin and meaning of ‘personality’ and why outward appearance does not define it.
  • Describe Freud’s three systems of personality: id, ego and superego, and their principles.
  • Explain Alfred Adler’s ideas: social interest, fictional finalism, striving for superiority, inferiority complex and creative self.
  • Compare Freud and Adler and relate personality to workplace behaviour.

Important definitions

Personality

From Latin persona, meaning mask. A set of qualities that make an individual distinct: emotional qualities, attitudes, values, beliefs, motivations, communication skills, ways of thinking and behaviour. Personality is an ‘inside thing’.

Id

The reservoir of instinctive energy; the true psychic reality and matrix of the ego and superego; a world of subjective experience that operates on the pleasure principle and cannot tolerate tension.

Ego

The executive of the personality and the organised portion of the id; follows the reality principle, differentiates memory and image and mediates between instincts and the environment.

Superego

The moral guardian or moral agency; the internal representative of good and evil; strives for perfection rather than pleasure and opposes both id and ego.

Fictional finalism

Adler’s idea that humans are guided by hypotheses or ‘fictions’ that cannot be tested (e.g. ‘honesty is the best policy’, ‘the end justifies the means’) but help them deal with reality.

Inferiority complex / striving for superiority

Every human feels some inferiority and compensates by striving for superiority, i.e. perfection.

Core concepts

What personality is not

Good looks, dress or fluency are only outward features. Shakespeare: “One may smile and smile and be a villain.” Two people who look identical may think very differently.

Why personality matters at work

  • Helps understand the mindset of people from different backgrounds and faiths.
  • Develops flexibility, persuasion, compassion and diplomacy.
  • Builds competence and career success; explains why people react the way they do.

Freud’s three systems

  • Id – pleasure principle; does not distinguish right from wrong; relieves tension through reflex action (sneezing, blinking) and primary process / wish fulfilment (e.g. a hungry person imagines food; repressed wishes appear in dreams – Interpretation of Dreams).
  • Ego – reality principle; asks whether an experience is true or false; finds realistic ways (how to get food); mediates between id and environment.
  • Superego – moral guardian; ideal rather than real; persuades the ego to substitute moralistic goals for realistic goals; inhibits impulses.

Every action passes through these three layers. “The child is the father of the man”: childhood habits and social influences continue; thwarted psychological growth can lead to introversion, pessimism or depression.

Alfred Adler’s theory (opposed Freud)

  • Humans are primarily social creatures, not sexual ones; social interest motivates behaviour.
  • Personality is shaped by expectations of the future, not only past experience.
  • Fictional finalism guides conduct.
  • Striving for superiority = striving for perfection; the inferiority complex drives compensation.
  • Humans identify with others (Freud’s term for imitation).
  • Each person has a unique style of life; “Le style, c’est l’homme” – the style is the man.
  • Masculine protest: both men and women seek power.
  • People can become a creative self and socially useful.
  • Example: Mariyappan Thangavelu, Paralympic gold medallist, compensated for a damaged leg. Adler himself was a sickly child who became a doctor.

Theories, principles, models and contributors

Freud’s psychoanalytic structure of personality — Sigmund Freud

Personality = id + ego + superego. Behaviour results from their interaction, often at an unconscious level.

Individual psychology — Alfred Adler

Social interest, fictional finalism, striving for superiority, creative self.

Examples and applications

Example

A hungry person imagines food (id, wish fulfilment); the ego plans how to get food; the superego checks whether it is morally right.

Example

A gruff person may hide great kindness; a smiling person may be cruel.

Example

Mariyappan Thangavelu turned a physical weakness into Paralympic success.

Summary

Personality (from persona, mask) is the inner set of qualities, attitudes, values and behaviours that make people distinct. Freud explained it through the id (pleasure), ego (reality) and superego (perfection). Adler, opposing Freud, stressed social interest, fictional finalism and striving for superiority, with the possibility of becoming a creative self.

Practice questions

Q1 MCQ The id operates on the:
  1. Reality principle
  2. Pleasure principle
  3. Moral principle
  4. Social principle
Answer: B
Why: The ego follows reality; the superego follows perfection.
Q2 MCQ Who proposed ‘fictional finalism’?
  1. Freud
  2. Hume
  3. Adler
  4. Shakespeare
Answer: C
Why: Alfred Adler.
Q3 MSQ Select the correct statements about the superego.
  1. It is the moral guardian
  2. It strives for perfection rather than pleasure
  3. It supports only the id
  4. It opposes both id and ego
Answer: A, B, D
Why: It does not support the id.
Q4 T/F Personality is derived from the Latin word meaning ‘mind’.
Answer: False
Why: It comes from persona, meaning mask.
Q5 Fill The executive of the personality is the ______.
Answer: ego
Why: It is also the organised portion of the id.
Q6 Short State two differences between Freud and Adler.
Answer: Freud stresses instincts and the past; Adler stresses social interest and future expectations.
Why: Adler also introduced the creative self.
