Lecture 21: Communication Breakdown Part I

Week 5 · Course book PDF pages 322–339 (printed pages 321–338)

Learning objectives

  • Define communication barrier or breakdown with the lecture’s examples.
  • Classify barriers: at the source, between people, and in the organisation.
  • Explain source barriers: faltering with words, uncertain content, background, wrong inference, perception, rigid categories, frozen evaluation, allness.
  • Explain interpersonal barriers: limited vocabulary, emotions, distraction, poor listening, selectivity, noise, verbal–non-verbal imbalance, semantic gap.

Important definitions

Communication barrier / breakdown

Anything that blocks, restricts or interferes with the smooth flow of a message so that it fails to achieve its purpose.

Homophily

A common frame of reference between sender and receiver (two doctors, two engineers).

Wrong inference

Drawing a mistaken conclusion, like mistaking a rope for a snake at dusk.

Frozen evaluation

Believing a past situation will repeat unchanged.

Allness (categorical thinking)

Using ‘all’ and ‘every’; believing one knows everything or that everyone is against one.

Communication selectivity

Picking out only the part of a message that interests you and ignoring the rest.

Semantic gap

A language or meaning gap caused by different uses of words.

Core concepts

Illustrative cases

  • A. G. Gardiner’s On Saying Please: the lift man throws out a passenger who says ‘top’ without ‘please’.
  • Crying baby: bottle offered, but the bed was wet.
  • Train ticket: date changes after 12 midnight; passenger missed the train.
  • Doctor’s ‘SOS’ misread: it means take as needed.
  • E-mail to a foreign friend: too long or too short, no reply.

Three kinds of breakdown

  1. At the source (sender).
  2. Between people (interpersonal).
  3. In the organisation (discussed in later lectures).

Remedy: find the cause, choose the best solution, get feedback.

Breakdown at the source

  • Faltering for words (leave applications, in an age of channels).
  • Uncertain content about why you are writing or speaking.
  • Different backgrounds (a book unread by the listener); lack of homophily.
  • Wrong inferences (rope or snake).
  • Different perceptions.
  • Rigid categories (‘I know nanotechnology; nothing new’).
  • Frozen evaluation (reaching early because you missed the train once).
  • Categorical thinking / allness (‘everyone is against me’).

“Change is the law of nature.”

Breakdown between people

  • Different perceptions of reality.
  • Limited vocabulary and multiple meanings (‘you are very fast’).
  • Emotional outbursts and tiredness.
  • Distraction from gadgets (laptop, phone, colleagues).
  • Poor listening; noise from adjacent rooms.
  • Communication selectivity (answering only a phrase in an interview).
  • Noise (also used as an excuse).
  • Imbalance between verbal and non-verbal messages.
  • Cultural differences (Kenya: son-in-law and mother-in-law do not face each other; silence).
  • Language as code: Mark Twain – the difference between the right word and the almost right word is that between lightning and a lightning bug.
  • Semantic gap: ‘loyal’ misused for ‘kind’; sidewalk (US), pavement (UK), platform (India); ‘table a motion’ – UK: bring up for discussion; US: postpone.

Lecture 22: Communication Breakdown Part II

Week 5 · Course book PDF pages 340–353 (printed pages 339–352)

Learning objectives

  • List the barriers to communication in organisations.
  • Explain transfer stations, media richness, jargon, physical distractions, ethics and misquoting.
  • Apply the remedies: non-judgmental language, empathy, listening, audience-centred messages, open climate, fewer levels, feedback.
  • Recall the lecture’s examples and Whitman’s quotation.

Important definitions

Transfer station

An intermediate level through which a message passes in a hierarchy; at each station the message may be reframed and distorted.

Media richness

The variety of channels available; the lecture stresses choosing the channel suited to the receiver’s background and facilities.

Lean media

Channels or messages for technical and objective information, which people often attend to least.

Proper channel

Following the prescribed route of reporting rather than approaching the top directly.

Misquoting

Reporting what another said inaccurately; an ethical lapse.

Audience-centred approach

Viewing the message from the receiver’s point of view (‘you’ attitude).

Core concepts

Organisational barriers

  • Organisational structure: tall hierarchy, many units; ‘proper channel’ restricts upward communication.
  • Fear of superior’s perception – juniors withhold information.
  • Negative attitudes of employees and managers destroy candour.
  • Status gap between senior and junior; lack of trust; grapevine functions as a ‘purgatory force’ (release).
  • Poor communication skills make peers avoid talking.
  • Groups and subgroups based on religion, faith, interest threaten channels.
  • Too many transfer stations distort messages: ‘the message sent is not always the message received’ (example: ‘a friend is passing away’ became ‘he is no more’).
  • Wrong choice of media, ignoring the receiver’s facilities; information overload.
  • Technical jargon used with non-experts.
  • Physical distractions: noise, poor acoustics, lighting, climate, power cuts, venue.
  • Ethical issues: misquotes, far-fetched predictions, rumours that go viral.

Overcoming the barriers

  • Be non-judgmental; select words carefully so they are not open to another meaning.
  • Put yourself in the other’s shoes; be empathetic; avoid unnecessary assumptions.
  • Be a good listener.
  • Avoid jargon; keep sentences and paragraphs short; consider cultural bias.
  • Keep non-verbal behaviour consistent with words.
  • Adopt an audience-centred approach.
  • Create an open communication climate.
  • Reduce the number of levels; use direct communication when important.
  • Seek immediate feedback.
  • Commit to ethical communication; avoid extreme statements; create lean, efficient messages.
  • Select the most suitable medium for the audience background.

The assumption story

A woman waiting at a bus stop smiled at a man's baby; he asked “Are you waiting for me?” — a wrong assumption. She replied that she had a baby at home. People misconstrue messages because they do not understand others’ assumptions and perceptions.

Walt Whitman (“Song of Myself”): “I celebrate myself, and sing myself, and what I assume you shall assume, for every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you.”

Lecture 23: Advanced Writing Skills

Week 5 · Course book PDF pages 354–375 (printed pages 353–374)

Learning objectives

  • Explain why writing matters and the features of advanced writing (conceptualise, research, draft, revise, reader-centred).
  • Differentiate informative and persuasive writing.
  • Apply directness, simple words and short sentences, and outlining.
  • List the nine writing patterns with examples.

Important definitions

Advanced writing

Professional, technical and scientific writing (proposals, reports, technical descriptions, business letters) based on a concept, research, drafting and revision, and centred on the reader.

Reader-centred writing

Writing planned from the reader’s point of view; it uses the ‘you’ tone.

Informative writing

Writing that conveys information as it is (e.g. stock verification form).

Persuasive / argumentative writing

Writing that explains, contradicts, counters arguments and convinces, with an assertive tone (thesis, dissertation).

Outline

A tentative plan with main topics, sub-topics and supporting points, like the tailor’s cutting plan.

Directness

Expressing the point plainly, without circumlocution.

Core concepts

Why writing matters

  • Francis Bacon: “Reading maketh a full man, conference a ready man, and writing an exact man.”
  • Reveals personality, grasp of language and reasoning in your absence.
  • Serves as a record (now through word processing and databases).
  • Professional work needs proposals, reports, manuals, technical descriptions and letters (credit, collection, complaint, adjustment).

Stages of advanced writing

  1. Conceptualise – have an idea; testify with truth and objectivity.
  2. Research – books and papers to support the concept.
  3. Draft – structure suited to report, letter, proposal or description.
  4. Revise – add, delete, check against the audience.
  5. Check that the writing is reader-centric.

Ask first: Why am I writing and for whom? Aims: express, inform, convince, persuade.

Style rules

  • Directness – plain, candid language, no circumlocution.
  • Short sentences (6–8 words are simple; 20–30 words are hard).
  • Familiar words; avoid jargon with lay readers; avoid pompous or wordy language; do not sacrifice meaning for brevity.
  • Make an outline; ensure unity, coherence and logic.
  • Put the verb early: “Please submit the report to me no later than 5 p.m. tomorrow” (better than “You will do me a personal favour…”).
  • Keep SVA (subject–verb agreement) and active voice; use ‘you’ tone.
  • Alexander Pope: “True ease in writing comes from art, not chance, as those move easiest who have learned to dance.”

Nine writing patterns

  1. Direct plan – good or routine news first: “Congratulations! Your proposal…”.
  2. Indirect plan – bad news: buffer, reasons, negative in the middle, positive ending (job-rejection example).
  3. Order of importance – most important reason first (recommendation letter).
  4. Chronology – time sequence (resume, incident report).
  5. Problem–solution.
  6. Causal – cause to effect or the reverse (Pali losing speakers).
  7. Spatial – by geography (sales north, south, east, west).
  8. Structural – by parts or structure (bonus, hike, compensation; course objective).
  9. Topical – breaks topics into subtopics.

Lecture 24: Principles of Business Writing

Week 5 · Course book PDF pages 376–392 (printed pages 375–391)

Learning objectives

  • List the 5 Cs of business writing: clarity, courtesy, consideration, conciseness, cordiality.
  • Explain how to achieve clarity, conciseness, courtesy and consideration with before-and-after examples.
  • Recall the quotations of T. S. Eliot and Oscar Wilde and the maxim ‘express, not impress’.
  • Rewrite negative and wordy sentences using the you-attitude.

Important definitions

Clarity

Writing that is unambiguous, plain and easily understood so that action can follow.

Courtesy

Respect shown to the reader in tone and word choice, even when information is dry.

Consideration

Writing from the reader’s viewpoint (the ‘you’ approach), so no one is hurt.

Conciseness

Being brief without sacrificing meaning; removing redundant words and flabby phrases.

Cordiality

Warmth and goodwill sustained in writing, since business is dealing with people.

Foggy writing

Unclear, cloudy messages produced by unskilled writers who have not learnt to communicate clearly.

Core concepts

The 5 Cs

  1. Clarity – ambiguity is an unwelcome guest in business.
  2. Courtesy
  3. Consideration
  4. Conciseness
  5. Cordiality

Goal: express, not impress; impress through simplicity and clarity.

Clarity

  • Unskilled writers produce foggy messages; use familiar words.
  • Example: “Employees have not been made sufficiently aware of the potentially adverse consequences…” → “Warn your employees about these chemicals.”
  • It is better to use a few more words than fewer words that are difficult.
  • T. S. Eliot: “Do not use big words too big for the subject; do not say ‘infinitely’ when you mean ‘very’.”

Conciseness: shorter expressions

  • a number of → many
  • at your earliest possible convenience → soon
  • fullest possible extent → fully
  • it would be unreasonable to assume → I assume
  • “There are three things I want you to do” → “I want you to do three things.”
  • Remove needless repetitions and redundant phrases; avoid excessive noun habit and pompous words; revise last. “Brevity is the soul of wit.”

Courtesy and consideration: rewriting

  • “You cannot meet the director; don’t you know he is out of station?” → “Thanks for your interest; today he is out of station. We will call you when he returns.”
  • “Will you send me 125 geysers at the official rate?” → “We require 125 geysers and expect to buy at the official rate.”
  • “The executive meeting is postponed.” → “We regret the inconvenience; the meeting has been postponed for unavoidable reasons.”
  • “You must know that PAN alone can make online tax procedure easy.” → “We would appreciate your PAN number; it will make tax procedure smoother.”
  • “Your project is returned; no more funds this year.” → “Thanks for submitting; we regret we lack funds this year, but will let you know once funds are available.”
  • Complaint about ACs: “We have received several complaints about the ACs supplied by us; we look forward to knowing what can be done.” (you-attitude)
  • Put the negative in the middle and end positively; give hope.

Cordiality

  • Avoid negative starts: “You are not a member, we cannot entertain you” → “We entertain our members; we expect you to join.”
  • “Your report is unsatisfactory” → “We appreciate a detailed report.”
  • “You should not ignore the official instructions” → “All of us follow the official timing: reports are due Friday.”
  • Oscar Wilde: “If you pretend to be good, the world takes you very seriously.” Keep hope alive, since a bad deal with a customer can cost an organisation heavily.

Lecture 25: Types of Business Writing Part I

Week 5 · Course book PDF pages 393–410 (printed pages 392–409)

Learning objectives

  • List the types of business writing: letters, reports, memos, letter reports, circulars, e-mails, technical proposals, research papers.
  • Define a memo, state its purposes, tone and format.
  • Differentiate memo, memo report, letter report and circular.
  • Describe the types of circulars.

Important definitions

Memo (inter-office memorandum)

A short, informal piece of business writing circulated within an organisation to confirm, suggest, inform, explain, announce, report or warn.

Memo report

A memo written in report form for a longer explanation.

Letter report

A report written in business-letter format; less informal than a memo, longer (about 2–3 pages), may go inside or outside the organisation, with an inside address, salutation and complimentary close.

Circular

A document circulated within (or sometimes outside) an organisation to inform, create awareness or announce policy, products or programmes.

cc (carbon copy)

A line at the end on the left, listing persons who receive a copy.

Follow the standards and practices of your organisation.

Purposes of a memo

  • Confirm (meeting outcomes)
  • Suggest action
  • Inform / explain (why something went wrong)
  • Announce policies, hikes, DA, products
  • Request / report information
  • Warn (late coming, misuse of photocopier) without offending, because the tone is informal

A memo is not always a threat.

Format of a memo

  • Letterhead (organisation name) at the top centre.
  • To and From on the left; From shows the designation, not the name.
  • Reference number and date on the right.
  • Subject line in the middle, explicit.
  • No salutation and no complimentary close; direct, short, specific, conversational, informal.
  • Body: problem → explanation → action required.
  • Writer’s name at the end (right); cc on the left.

Sample memo: from Dean (Admission) to all heads about dissatisfactory campus placement; proposes faculty development training and meeting heads; cc Dean Academics.

Letter report

  • Letterhead, date, inside address, subject, formal salutation (‘Dear Sir’), introduction, findings, complimentary close, signature, enclosures (with number of pages).
  • Longer than a memo (1 page memo, 2–3 pages letter report); includes discussion and illustrations; less formal than a full formal report.
  • Example: report on a faculty development programme.

Circular

  • Purpose: inform, create awareness, matters of general interest.
  • Use a memo for people within the organisation; use a circular for larger audiences including outside.
  • Structure: introduction, details, relevant information; short unless required.
  • Letterhead on top centre, ‘Circular’, content, signature.
  • Revise carefully for spelling and grammar because reputation is at stake.

Summary

Business writing includes letters, reports, memos, letter reports, circulars, e-mails, proposals and research papers. A memo is a short, informal intra-office document with letterhead, To/From (designation), reference and date, subject line and body but no salutation or close. A letter report is a longer, letter-format report that may go outside; a circular informs larger audiences and has four types.

Practice questions

  1. Subject line
  2. Salutation
  3. Date
  4. Reference number

Answer: B

  2. Public
  3. Official
  4. Private diary
Answer: A, B, C
Why: Partnership/company is also a type.
Q4 T/F A letter report is always shorter than a memo.
Answer: False
Why: It is longer (2–3 pages).
Q5 Fill A memo is also called an ______ memorandum.
Answer: inter-office
Why: It circulates within the organisation.
Q6 Short State two purposes of a memo.
Answer: To confirm a meeting outcome and to announce a policy (also suggest, explain, report, warn).
Why: Tone remains informal.
