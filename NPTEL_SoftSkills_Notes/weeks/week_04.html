Lecture 16: Negotiation Skills I

Week 4 · Course book PDF pages 240–254 (printed pages 239–253)

Learning objectives

  • Define negotiation and list its ingredients.
  • Differentiate formal and informal negotiation.
  • Explain factors affecting negotiation: place, time, atmosphere, subjective factors, fear, influence.
  • State the qualities of a negotiator and the stages of negotiation; define BATNA, WATNA and ZOPA.

Important definitions

Negotiation (Alan Fowler)

A form of meeting or discussion in which the parties use argument and persuasion to reach an agreed decision or action. (The transcript spells the name ‘Alan Flower’.)

Negotiation (process view)

A process of combining conflicting positions into a common position, turning conflict into agreement.

BATNA

Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: your fallback if the negotiation fails.

WATNA

Worst Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: the worst result that you must avoid accepting out of fear or influence.

ZOPA

Zone Of Possible Agreement: the range in which the parties’ interests overlap and agreement is possible.

Win-win situation

An agreement that benefits both parties.

Core concepts

Nature of negotiation

  • Arises when there is a conflict or deadlock or difference of opinion.
  • Needs two parties with interests; discussion must not be one-sided.
  • Works through persuasion and instruction, not authority.
  • Outcome should benefit both parties; sometimes lose-win, ideally win-win.
  • Examples: buying a car; employee seeking a raise from the boss.

Formal versus informal negotiation

  • Formal: pre-announced date, venue, time and agenda; parties know the subject; usually three people: two parties and a negotiator/observer.
  • Informal: not announced; casual, friendly; two persons; little preparation; position and familiarity can create pressure or deadlock; need extra restraint.

Factors affecting negotiation

  • Place: own place gives comfort, confidence and access to documents.
  • Time: mutually suitable; not imposed.
  • Atmosphere: free exchange; no threat; seek common ground.
  • Subjective factors: personal relationships, bias of observer, fear, influence and mutual obligation, personal ambition.
  • Past negotiations may help.
  • Kennedy: “Let us never negotiate out of fear, but let us never fear to negotiate.”

Qualities of a negotiator

  • Confidence from preparation; have a plan B.
  • Appearance – dress with dignity.
  • Outlook – flexible, ready to concede but never agree unless you have gained.
  • Honesty and reliability – know your subject and both sides’ strengths and weaknesses.
  • Competence – communication and reading meta-communication and facial reactions.
  • Flexibility and positivity; no agreement can be totally fair.

Stages and tactics

  • Preparation (study the issue, gather facts, know upper and lower limits) → Negotiation/interaction (simple language, stay cool, conducive atmosphere) → agreement.
  • Use polite language: “I think we need some more clarity in this regard”; “Can you repeat what you said earlier?”
  • Facts must be factual and consistent; many negotiations fail because of poor control of vocabulary.
  • Upper limit = how much you concede; lower limit = loss you can accept.
  • Prepare BATNA; avoid WATNA; aim for ZOPA and a win-win outcome.

Lecture 17: Negotiation Skills II

Week 4 · Course book PDF pages 255–269 (printed pages 254–268)

Learning objectives

  • Explain persuasion factors in negotiation: style, you-attitude, body language, summarising.
  • Describe the three phases: preparation, interaction (negotiation), implementation.
  • Distinguish red, blue and purple negotiation styles.
  • List negotiation approaches (collaborate, accommodate, compromise, control, avoid) and dos and don’ts.

Important definitions

You-attitude

Convincing the other party that they will benefit; telling them the ‘sunny side’ of the deal and letting them talk while you listen.

Sunny side of the deal

The advantages to the other party that you highlight to persuade them.

Red style

Self-centred, competitive negotiation style.

Blue style

Cooperative, less competitive style.

Purple style

A mix of red and blue; the recommended style.

Win-win / win-lose / lose-win

Possible outcomes of a negotiation; no negotiation gives a total win to one party.

Core concepts

Persuasion factors

  • Style: language, convincing approach, presenting strengths and weaknesses; each party presents its case first.
  • You-attitude: highlight the benefit to the other; combine talking and listening.
  • Body language: glances, eyes and face complement words.
  • Summarising: “I have said this, you have said this… something still needs discussion.”

Agreement is the culmination (“sum and substance”) of exchanged views.

Three phases of negotiation

  1. Preparation – plan, study pros and cons, know your strength and the other’s weakness, upper and lower limits, anticipate (plan B), discover the real issue, be realistic. Tim Hindle: it is almost impossible for a negotiator to do too much preparation.
  2. Interaction / negotiation – introductions (often by the observer), exchange initial views, test the initial position, look for common ground (ZOPA), small sacrifices, watch time, note down the points of agreement, control vocabulary.
  3. Implementation – decide roles after agreement and ensure the agreement sustains.

Negotiation styles by colour

  • Red: self-centred, competitive.
  • Blue: cooperative.
  • Purple: blend; best choice.

Approaches / qualities

  • Collaborate – avoid confrontation.
  • Accommodate – polite, allow more room, especially with other cultures.
  • Compromise – do not remain rigid.
  • Control – be forgiving, conceding, convincing; ask for clarification advantageously.
  • Avoid – keep emotion out of sight, avoid confrontational tone.

Dos and don’ts

  • Keep some key points in reserve; do not say everything at once.
  • Do not get personal; read the tone, not only the words.
  • Do not prolong discussion after agreement; sign at the right time and record points.
  • Acknowledge the other party’s cooperation.
  • Be ready for deadlock, defeat and lose-win outcomes; hide signs of loss and stay optimistic.
  • Allow other cultures more time and space.
  • “In business you do not get what you deserve, you get what you negotiate.”
  • Negotiation is cooperation, not competition.

Lecture 18: Culture as Communication

Week 4 · Course book PDF pages 270–288 (printed pages 269–287)

Learning objectives

  • Define culture and explain how it affects communication.
  • Explain cultural shock, diaspora and globalisation (GATT, NAFTA).
  • List the dimensions of culture: context, individualism, formality, communication style, time orientation.
  • Compare communication styles of Americans, Japanese, Arabs, Mexicans, Germans, Europeans and others, and cite translation blunders.

Important definitions

Culture

A combination of traits, behaviours, values, myths and traditions of a group; the totality of a group’s thoughts, experiences and patterns of behaviour and their contact with other cultures. It is learnt and dynamic.

Cultural shock

The feeling of isolation or being a loner that arises when one is unfamiliar with a different culture’s way of living and responding.

Global village

The world seen as interconnected through technology, trade and travel.

Diaspora

People of one country settled in another country.

Cultural sensitivity

Awareness and respect for differences between cultures when communicating.

Deference vs difference

The lecture advises showing deference (respect) rather than difference.

Core concepts

Why culture matters

  • We assume the world behaves as we do; exposure to other cultures shows differences.
  • Culture shapes eating, dress, vocabulary, festivals, thinking and identity; it gives a sense of belonging.
  • Globalisation: GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade) and NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement), IT and transport exchange cultures; workplaces are multicultural. Example: idli–dosa in the north.
  • Cultures are logical (a cat crossing the road is an omen to Indians) and dynamic; learn symbols, rituals, values, heroes and worships.
  • Components to understand: language, gestures, appearance, religion, philosophy, values, family customs, communication systems.
  • Example of cultural shock: the film/novel The Namesake (childbirth abroad alone).
  • Gandhi: different ways to reach God, but the aim is one.

Major dimensions of culture

  1. Context (high versus low)
  2. Individualism
  3. Formality
  4. Communication style
  5. Time orientation

Formality and style

  • Americans: casual, direct, first names or titles, uncomfortable with silence and delay, take words literally; ‘hamburger style’ criticism (praise bun, criticism meat, praise bun).
  • Europeans: formal; do not use first names without invitation.
  • Japan/China: use titles; Japanese treat card exchange and signing as ritual.
  • Mexicans: value courtesies and food before business; hurt if pushed to business.
  • Arabs, South Americans and Asians: friendship first; poetic and indirect language.
  • Germans: direct but understated; Nigerians: quiet and clear; Japanese: subtle.
  • Asians use silence to gain concessions.

Contracts and context

  • Americans (low context): contracts are specific and word-based.
  • Greeks: a formal statement.
  • Japanese: a statement of intention that can improve.
  • Mexicans: an artistic exercise achievable in an ideal world.
  • Arabs (high context): contract is insulting; stress non-verbal cues.

Space and time

  • US and Canada keep about five feet; this is too close or too far for others.
  • Boss in the corner (US) versus middle (Mexico).
  • North Americans treat time as a commodity; Asians deliberate long; some cultures are relaxed (an American waited two hours for a purchase).

Translation blunders

  • Swiss restaurant: “Our wines will make you forget everything / leave you nowhere.”
  • “Women are not allowed to have their children at the bar.”
  • “Our water has been passed by the manager himself.”

Understand the essence, not the literal words.

Summary

Culture is the learnt set of traits, values and behaviours of a group and strongly shapes communication. Globalisation brings cultural contact and, sometimes, cultural shock. Cultures differ in context, individualism, formality, communication style and time orientation; low-context Americans and Germans are direct and word-based, while high-context Arabs, Japanese and Asians stress relationship, silence and non-verbal cues. Respect, deference and learning are the keys to cross-cultural success.

Lecture 19: Communicating across Cultures

Week 4 · Course book PDF pages 289–304 (printed pages 288–303)

Learning objectives

  • Define communicating across cultures and Alfred G. Smith’s definition of culture.
  • Contrast low-context and high-context cultures.
  • Distinguish intercultural, cross-cultural and global communication contexts.
  • Explain ethnocentrism, stereotyping and the movement toward ethno-relativism; recall cultural examples.

Important definitions

Culture (Alfred G. Smith)

“Culture is a code we learn and share, and learning and sharing require communication.” Communication requires coding and symbols that must be learned and shared. (From Communication and Culture.)

Intercultural communication

Face-to-face interaction among people of diverse cultures (also between subcultures of one country).

Global context

Trans-border transfer of information by institutions and groups.

Cross-cultural communication

Comparing a phenomenon across cultures; problems arise when meanings differ.

Ethnocentrism

The belief that one’s own culture is the best.

Ethno-relativism

Accepting that cultures differ and adapting to them; reached through stages from denial to acceptance and adaptation.

Stereotyping

An oversimplified view of a culture or community, judging individuals by their group.

Core concepts

Low versus high context

  • Low context (North America, Scandinavia, France, Germany): logic, written contracts, words, analytical and action-oriented language, individualism, little role for silence and non-words.
  • High context (Japan, China, Arab countries): meaning from context, intuition, silence and non-verbal cues; contemplative; social harmony and saving face; prefer friendship to contract.
  • Example: a Japanese buyer paused, folded arms and closed eyes while the American seller grew impatient.

Communication contexts

  • International: face-to-face among diverse cultures.
  • Global: information across borders.
  • Cross-cultural: comparing across cultures.
  • Intercultural: interaction between people of different cultures, including subcultures (Indian Bengali versus Bangladeshi Bengali; American versus Indian English).

Cultural variations

  • Superstition: a worm in pulque is fortunate in Mexico; the Japanese Maneki neko (beckoning cat) attracts customers.
  • Numbers: 8 is lucky in China and Hong Kong; 4 sounds like death (shi) so no room 4 in hospitals.
  • Space: Arabs of the same sex stand closer; North Americans keep five feet.
  • Silence: Americans cannot bear long silence.
  • Colour, greeting and touch differ.
  • Saying no: USA/Canada shake head; Bulgaria nods up and down; Japan moves the right hand; Sicily raises the chin.
  • An Indian politely refusing food saying ‘no’ went hungry among Americans.
  • HP: French engineers wrote shorter e-mails, which Americans read as withholding information.
  • Mazda: casual baseball caps hurt Americans’ feelings.
  • Gifts: a clock in China invites evil; do not gift an Arab’s wife; a rose to a German lady may signal romance. ‘How was your weekend?’ may seem intrusive.
  • Gestures: Thatcher’s victory sign and the thumbs-up may be negative elsewhere; Asian men holding hands is friendly.

Variables to watch

Ethnocentrism, education, religious faith, country laws, language and use of space are variables in cross-cultural communication.

Bridging cultural gaps

  • Tolerance, empathy, patience; do not judge; give others space and time.
  • Avoid stereotypes (a basket of mangoes: not all are rotten).
  • Move from ethnocentrism to ethno-relativism through six stages (denial, defence, minimisation, acceptance, adaptation, integration), seeking commonalities.
  • Indians care about image; Mexicans and Asians about harmony and saving face.
  • Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam (“Ayam nijah paro veti…”): the world is one family.

Lecture 20: Organizational Communication

Week 4 · Course book PDF pages 305–321 (printed pages 304–320)

Learning objectives

  • Define organisation and organisational communication (William Scott).
  • State functions and objectives of organisational communication.

Important definitions

Organisation

A social unit of people structured and managed to meet a need or pursue collective goals.

Organisational communication (William Scott)

A process involving transmission and accurate replication of ideas, their dissemination, promotion and distribution, ensured by feedback, to elicit actions that accomplish the goals of the organisation.

Information Management System (IMS)

The core system by which information entering an organisation is managed and used.

Line management

People responsible for basic company activities such as manufacturing and sales.

Staff management

People who support line management with services such as accounting, distribution and personnel.

Grapevine

An informal channel of casual talk, whispers and rumours.

Crisis communication

Urgent communication among core managers when something goes wrong; combines persuasion with caution.

Core concepts

Why communication matters in an organisation

  • Carry out management, allocate jobs, disseminate information and clarify roles.
  • The communication environment shows a person’s calibre and is an asset.
  • Employees come from different backgrounds, ages, genders and faiths; communication must suit everyone.
  • Communication flows to bosses, groups and other departments.

Functions of communication

  1. Forecasting – what we want to do and where we want to be.
  2. Organising – distributing work and responsibilities among divisions (quality control, HR, accounts, inventory).
  3. Instructing / persuading – leader instructs, persuades or orders.
  4. Coordinating – across divisions.
  5. Controlling – the leader controls and is a role model.

Objectives

  • Improve job performance and update knowledge (workshops, conferences).
  • Create belonging; make employees feel heard and appreciated.
  • Convince employees about decisions; set daily, monthly and annual aims jointly.
  • Assign roles and empower employees with information.

Managerial roles

  • Interpersonal – talking with individuals and groups.
  • Informational – receiving and giving information; be like an ambassador who knows everything.
  • Decisional – listen to problems, interpret information and decide.

Maslow’s hierarchy at work

Physiological (food, shelter, salary) → safety/security → social (belonging, love, appreciation) → esteem/ego → self-actualisation (morality, creativity, spontaneity, purpose). “Ambition is everyone’s slave.”

McGregor’s two styles

  • Traditional (Theory X): workers need only basic needs; treated as labour; language of control.
  • Modern (Theory Y): balance of control and freedom; workers treated as humans with higher needs; language of persuasion; manager must understand their needs.

Communication flow and channels

  • Vertical (downward from top, upward), horizontal, crosswise (diagonal), and spiral for policy announcements (hike, bonus).
  • Line and staff management coordinate; staff support line.
  • Formal and informal channels (casual chat, grapevine). Managers may use the grapevine for warnings or to caution disgruntled employees; filter its information.
  • Koontz and O’Donnell: “The most effective communication results when managers utilise the informal organisational channels to supplement the formal organisational channels.”

Types of communication at workplace

  • Statutory – norms, terms and conditions, policies, bulletins.
  • Crisis management – urgent meetings of core members; persuasion and caution.

A satisfied employee is more productive than a dissatisfied one.

Summary

Organisational communication carries ideas through formal and informal channels so that goals are achieved. Its functions include forecasting, organising, instructing, coordinating and controlling; managers play interpersonal, informational and decisional roles. Understanding Maslow’s needs and McGregor’s styles helps leaders persuade rather than only control. Statutory and crisis communication, line–staff coordination and a careful use of the grapevine complete the picture.
