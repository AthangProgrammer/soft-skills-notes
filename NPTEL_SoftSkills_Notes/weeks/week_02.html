Lecture 6: Positive Thinking

Week 2 · Course book PDF pages 84–99 (printed pages 83–98)

Learning objectives

  • Define positive thinking and distinguish optimists from pessimists.
  • Explain Eric Berne’s four life positions (I’m OK, you’re OK, etc.).
  • Describe the Johari Window and its four areas, and how each area can be expanded.
  • State ways of developing positivism using examples from the lecture.

Important definitions

Positive thinking

Norman Vincent Peale (The Power of Positive Thinking): a positive mental attitude that firmly believes things will soon be better and so overcomes difficulty.

Optimist / pessimist

An optimist looks at the bright side (glass half full) and creates opportunities; a pessimist looks at the dark side (glass half empty) and finds negativity in every opportunity.

Johari Window

A model devised by American psychologists Joseph Luft and Harry Ingham in 1955 to illustrate and improve self-awareness and mutual understanding in a group through disclosure and feedback.

Open area

Known to the person and known to others; it can be expanded through open, positive communication, feedback and interaction.

Blind area

Not known to the person but known to others; reduced through non-judgmental feedback.

Hidden area

Known to the person but kept hidden from others; reduced through self-disclosure, which builds trust.

Unknown area

Unknown to the person and unknown to others (latent talents); uncovered through mutual or collective discovery.

Core concepts

Why positive thinking helps

  • A positive person believes ‘I can do it’ and so makes a good leader, entrepreneur and risk-taker.
  • One who never takes risks never gains.
  • William James: “We are only half awake” – we use only a small part of our physical and mental resources; discover your limits and hidden powers.
  • A new player who faces the game positively can win.

Eric Berne’s attitudes (life positions)

  • I’m not OK, you’re not OK – introvert; cannot proceed.
  • I’m not OK, you’re OK – negative.
  • I’m OK, you’re not OK – imposing (only he matters).
  • I’m OK, you’re OK – positive and co-operative; ideal for teamwork.

How to develop positivism

  • Believe in your abilities and be yourself; you have immense untapped potential.
  • Do not blindly imitate others; imitate only qualities that help you, without losing your own identity (story of the singer who hid her teeth).
  • Concentrate on your goals and follow the right action; Gita: “Karmanye vadhikaraste ma phaleshu kadachana”.
  • People change: Roosevelt was physically weak; Demosthenes stuttered yet became a great orator.
  • Dhammapada: “All that we are is the result of what we have thought.”

Johari Window in groups

When a new person enters a group, not all of his skills are known to others or to himself. Group members should help him expose them. The window has four quadrants (areas). Feedback and self-disclosure move information into the open area; the open area grows both horizontally and vertically. Managers can promote honest, non-judgmental feedback. Better self-awareness improves communication, relationships and productivity.

Lecture 7: Telephonic Communication Skills Part I

Week 2 · Course book PDF pages 100–114 (printed pages 99–113)

Learning objectives

  • Explain the importance of telephonic communication in life and business.
  • List the special features of telephonic communication: clarity, courtesy, consideration, brevity.
  • Apply the etiquette of making, receiving, transferring and closing calls.
  • Handle complaints and voicemail professionally and avoid negative language.

Important definitions

Telephonic communication

Sending and receiving messages through telephone instruments; the fastest and a low-cost means of contact across distance.

Netiquette versus etiquette

Netiquette is etiquette for internet communication; the lecturer notes that we forget basic etiquettes in the age of netiquettes.

Voicemail

A recorded message left when the call cannot be completed; it should be complete.

Core concepts

Why telephones matter

  • Bridge distance and time; fast, saves travel, cost-effective.
  • Used to place orders, book tickets, give feedback, check quality, contact students, sellers and distributors.
  • The reputation of an organisation depends on how its people communicate by phone.

Limitations

No face-to-face contact and no immediate visual feedback; the only cue is the voice. Video-calls are costly and not for everyone. Voice must carry assurance, sincerity, intimacy and enthusiasm.

Features of telephonic communication

  1. Clarity – the first requirement.
  2. Courtesy – right time, right address and tone; you cannot see the other person.
  3. Consideration – empathy; the other party may be in a noisy or congested place; do not make them repeatedly ask “can you be loud?”.
  4. Brevity – be brief and specific because someone is paying and time is limited.

While making a call

  • Decide whether the call is necessary; if it can be avoided, avoid it.
  • Place your own call instead of asking a secretary.
  • Plan an agenda (purpose; keep pen and paper) to avoid repeated calls.
  • Identify yourself and your affiliation (name, department, institute) and state the purpose.
  • Address the person by name; avoid small talk such as “How is the weather?” in official calls.
  • Speak clearly at a moderate volume; imagine you are speaking face to face.
  • Close politely (“It was nice talking to you…”); never end abruptly.
  • If the call is cut or you must stop, say you will call back, and call back when promised; leave a complete voicemail if needed.

While receiving a call

  • Answer immediately, not later than three rings.
  • Stop other conversation; identify yourself at once (“I am … from …, how may I help you?”).
  • Respond positively, even if you dislike the caller; “good gestures always mean good business”.
  • Take the message carefully: note caller’s name (verify spelling), number and message and pass it on.
  • To transfer a call, explain why and ask consent (“May I put you on hold?”).
  • For an angry complainant: stay calm, listen, note the concern, assure a response, and let the caller hang up first.

Language to use and avoid

  • Avoid negatives such as “No, he is not here”, “I cannot do that”, “You have to…”, “Why did you not…?”.
  • Use “Tell me what I can do for you”, “I would like you to…”, “Will/would you please…”.

Lecture 8: Telephonic Communication Skills Part II

Week 2 · Course book PDF pages 115–126 (printed pages 114–125)

Learning objectives

  • Explain voice mail, its advantages and limits, and how to leave and answer a voice message.
  • State mobile (cell phone) etiquette.
  • Differentiate teleconferencing (audio) and video conferencing, including the three types of video conferencing.
  • Know when teleconferencing is ineffective and the cautions for video conferencing.

Important definitions

Voice mail

A recorded-message facility that acts as a substitute for short memos and short phone calls, solves time-zone difficulties and reduces paperwork; messages are digitised and stored.

Automated attendant

A menu system (e.g. “for account balance press 1”) through which callers get information or record messages.

Teleconferencing

A telephonic meeting with more than two people, requiring a leader or facilitator; an alternative to a face-to-face meeting. Can be audio or video.

Video conferencing

Audio plus video link so that communication appears face to face; a tool of business and management for people separated by time and distance.

Core concepts

Voice mail: use and etiquette

  • Advantages: substitute for short memos and phone calls; solves time-zone difficulties; reduces interoffice paperwork (paperless office); easy and low cost; can be replayed; can carry goodwill messages.
  • Limitation: no immediate feedback; automated responses are general, not specific.
  • When leaving a message: state your name clearly, give the point of the message, your phone number and the time/date you are available; avoid interrupting.
  • When replying: reply quickly; listen again and write your response before returning the call.

Cell phone etiquette

  • Speak in a low, modulated tone; do not shout (the Delhi bus example).
  • Switch off or use silent mode in meetings, conferences, theatres, cinemas, temples, churches and classrooms; send a short message if a call is urgent.
  • Do not call at odd hours unless urgent.
  • Avoid talking in poor signal; suspend and resume later.
  • Do not multitask (laptop plus call plus instructions) at the cost of courtesy; dial correctly to avoid wrong numbers.
  • Phones are sensitive, so privacy is limited; keep private calls separate from official ones.

Teleconferencing

  • Needs a leader/facilitator; can be arranged at short notice; connects geographically dispersed people; saves travel cost.
  • Audio conferences give good sound quality when conference rooms and microphones are used.
  • Ineffective for negotiations and decision-making because facial expressions and gestures cannot be read; little scope for secondary conversation; poor signals can defeat the purpose.

Video conferencing

  • Emerging major tool for business and management; cheaper than journeys; top executives use it for decisions.
  • Three types: one-way video and one-way audio; two-way audio with one-way video; two-way video and two-way audio (e.g. Skype, news-channel discussions).
  • Cautions: check microphones and camera; introduce yourself (name, post, affiliation); control body movements; avoid parallel whispers; look at the camera; avoid distracting clothes and excessive movement.

Man is the master

“Technology should not become the master; man should become the master.” Be sensitive to others’ reactions, culture, time and energy.

Lecture 9: Communicating Without Words

Week 2 · Course book PDF pages 127–147 (printed pages 126–146)

Learning objectives

  • Define non-verbal communication and explain why it matters.
  • Quote the Birdwhistell and Mehrabian percentages.
  • List the types of non-verbal communication: kinesics, proxemics, chronemics, paralanguage, haptics, meta-communication.
  • Describe the forms of kinesics: personal appearance, facial expression, eye contact, movement, posture and gesture, with cultural differences.

Important definitions

Non-verbal communication

Communication without words, through symbols and cues such as expression, gesture, posture and eye behaviour. It is spontaneous and unintentional, and it supplements and complements verbal communication.

Kinesics

The study of body movement or body language; includes personal appearance, facial expression, eye contact, movement, posture and gesture.

Proxemics

Use of space and distance in communication (from proximity).

Chronemics

The role of time in communication.

Paralanguage

How words are spoken, beyond the words themselves (voice qualities).

Haptics

Communication through touch.

Posture

Positioning: how you sit, stand and hold your arms and shoulders.

Core concepts

Verbal and non-verbal proportions

  • Ray Birdwhistell: 35% of communication is verbal and 65% non-verbal.
  • Mehrabian: 7% verbal (words), 38% vocal, and the remaining (55%) non-verbal.
  • One study counts more than 6 lakh non-verbal cues, so they are categorised.

Nature of non-verbal cues

  • Cannot substitute words (e.g. ‘happiness’, ‘silence’) but have more impact because they are spontaneous.
  • They are unintentional and unstructured; posed expressions become stereotyped (as with actors).
  • Work at two levels: the verbal level (words) and the non-verbal level.
  • People are communicating all the time; even silence has meaning (differs across cultures).
  • Symbols and emoticons in chat and email are non-verbal cues.

Types of non-verbal communication

  1. Kinesics – body language
  2. Proxemics – space
  3. Chronemics – time
  4. Paralanguage – voice
  5. Haptics – touch
  6. Meta-communication

Kinesics: sign language (posed, stereotyped), action language (body behaviour) and object language.

Personal appearance and face

  • The face is a junction of emotions; ‘face is the index of the mind’, but ‘a false face must have a false heart’ when one manipulates.
  • Liars find it hard to hide body language (child with clamped hands in pockets).
  • Dress should be clean, ironed and suitable; it creates the first impression. It need not be expensive.
  • Freud: “He who has eyes to see and ears to hear can convince himself that no mortal can keep a secret.”
  • Chesterton: “There are no uninteresting topics, there are uninteresting people.”

Eye contact

  • Eyes are ‘the mirror of the heart and windows of the soul’; they show sincerity, honesty and conviction and establish rapport and credibility.
  • Culture-specific: Arabs, Latin Americans and southern Europeans gaze at the face; Asians, Indians, Pakistanis and northern Europeans prefer peripheral gaze or none (Harper, Wiens and Matarazzo).
  • Eye contact should be neither a gaze nor a stare.
  • Herodotus: “Men trust their ears less than their eyes.”
  • Glowering looks and checking watches show the audience is bored.

Movement and posture

  • Do not rush; avoid excessive or distracting movement; move gracefully.
  • Dale Carnegie: people form an impression from how you dress, walk, sit and stand.
  • Posture: do not recline; avoid hands in pockets, clamped hands, playing with key rings, shuffling.

Gestures

  • Gestures back up language; do not repeat the same gesture (e.g. thumping the desk).
  • Types: enumerative and symbolic gestures.
  • Limp hands – boredom; flat hands – silent questions; open hands – welcome; clenched hands – tension/frustration; fist – determination; crossed arms – negative; crossed legs – insulting to the audience.
  • Handshakes: glove handshake (politician); dead-fish handshake (formal/unwilling).
  • Gestures differ by culture: ‘come here’ in the US may mean ‘go away’ in Italy.

Summary

Non-verbal communication complements words and is spontaneous and unintentional. Birdwhistell put it at 65% and Mehrabian at 55% with 38% vocal. Its categories are kinesics, proxemics, chronemics, paralanguage, haptics and meta-communication. Kinesics covers appearance, face, eyes, movement, posture and gestures, all of which vary by culture and must be controlled to create a favourable impression.

Lecture 10: Paralanguage

Week 2 · Course book PDF pages 148–167 (printed pages 147–166)

Learning objectives

  • Define paralanguage and list the features of voice: quality, volume, rate, pitch, pronunciation, pauses and silence.
  • Identify voice faults: throatiness, breathiness, nasalisation, harshness, muffling and hoarseness.
  • State the ideal speaking rate and how to improve articulation.
  • Explain intonation, stress, pauses, silence and the quotations of Mark Twain and Pythagoras.

Important definitions

Paralanguage

The study of how words are spoken: the vocal characteristics (quality, volume, rate, pitch, pronunciation, pauses) that add meaning beyond the words.

Articulation

Producing speech sounds clearly through the speech organs; clarity is the hallmark of communication.

Audibility

The ability of the listener to hear the speaker; related to volume.

Intonation

The rises and falls in the voice.

Inflection

Changes in pitch produced by vibration of the vocal cords while speaking; they convey feelings.

Pause

A short silence in speech that separates units of thought; a longer pause becomes silence.

Received Pronunciation (RP)

The standard pronunciation recommended for clear, intelligible speech instead of imitating Americans or Britons.

Core concepts

Why voice matters

  • Everyone’s voice is unique and carries intentional and unintentional emotions.
  • The quality of voice can be improved by training (Lincoln and Churchill had voice problems).
  • Speech production passes through stages starting with the articulatory–respiratory process using lips, teeth, nasal passage, windpipe.
  • English as a second or third language causes translation lapses and pronunciation problems.

Features of voice

  1. Quality – check audibility; neither too loud nor too slow.
  2. Volume – degree of loudness; adjust to audience size and room acoustics (no shouting for 20–30 people).
  3. Rate – adjust to the audience’s background.
  4. Pitch – variations and inflections.
  5. Pronunciation.
  6. Pauses and silence.

Rate of speech

  • Ideal average: 140–150 words per minute (the lecturer also advises at least 130–150).
  • Too fast: you forget and run out of material; too slow: boredom. Follow the ‘madhyam marga’ (middle path).
  • Imagine an audience of ordinary, moderately educated people.

Faults of voice

  • Throatiness – deep resonance in the throat.
  • Breathiness – too much air through the vocal cords.
  • Nasalisation – air released through the nose.
  • Harshness – uninhibited flow of air.
  • Muffling – lazy lip and jaw movement.
  • Hoarseness – inflammation of the larynx; a medical problem, consult a speech therapist.

Improving the voice

  • Speak distinctly; avoid chopping, slurring and mumbling (‘gonna’, ‘wanna’, ‘dunno’, ‘otta’, ‘dint’) and fillers (ah, um).
  • Record yourself on a mobile phone and rehearse.
  • Use a dictionary, thesaurus and phonetic notation for difficult words.
  • Follow Received Pronunciation; learn from good news anchors; listen actively.
  • Vary pitch to avoid monotony; use intonation.

Stress and intonation

  • Content words (nouns, adjectives, verbs, adverbs) take stress; articles, prepositions, conjunctions, auxiliary verbs and personal pronouns usually do not.
  • Stress the correct syllable (use a dictionary).
  • Falling tone for commands and wh-questions (‘Bring me a cup of tea’, ‘Where had you been?’).
  • Rising tone for requests and yes/no questions and question tags.
  • Modulate voice to express intended meaning (the late student seeking permission).

Pauses and silence

  • Pauses separate units of thought; begin and end a speech with a pause.
  • Mark Twain: “The right word may be effective, but no word was ever as effective as a rightly timed pause.”
  • A longer pause becomes silence; silence is a non-verbal cue with different meanings across cultures (approval, disapproval, disagreement).
  • Silence helps thinking, introspection and decision-making for both speaker and listener.
  • Pythagoras: “Silence is better than unmeaning words.”

Summary

Paralanguage covers voice quality, volume, rate, pitch, pronunciation and pauses. Speakers should speak at about 140–150 words per minute, vary pitch to avoid monotony, articulate each sound distinctly, follow Received Pronunciation, stress content words and use pauses and silence intentionally. Voice faults such as throatiness, breathiness, nasalisation, harshness, muffling and hoarseness can be reduced by training, except hoarseness, which is medical.
