Lecture 36: Structure of Reports: Part-I

Week 8 · Course book PDF pages 576–592 (printed pages 575–591)

Learning objectives

  • State the three structural divisions of a technical report.
  • List the items of the prefatory material (front matter) in order.
  • Describe the cover page, title page, frontispiece, copyright notice and forwarding letter.
  • Differentiate preface and foreword; write an acknowledgement.
  • Prepare a table of contents and list of illustrations; differentiate abstract, summary and executive summary.

Important definitions

Prefatory material (front matter)

Items preceding the main text of a report: cover, frontispiece, title page, copyright notice, forwarding letter, preface, acknowledgement, table of contents, list of illustrations, abstract.

Cover page

Outer page on thick paper bearing ‘Report on’, title, report number, writer, ‘prepared for’, organisation and month/year.

Title page

Page after the cover with the title (and subtitle), prepared for/by, ‘approved by’ and organisation and date.

Frontispiece

A thin protective page, or illustration, facing the title page, giving a window display of the report to create curiosity; used in bound reports.

Copyright notice

A statement on the back of the title page such as ‘© Amit Jain 2017. All rights reserved. No part of this report may be reproduced…without written permission’.

Forwarding letter / letter of transmittal

A letter by the controlling officer forwarding the report through proper channel; includes authorisation, subject, purpose, scope, limitations and acknowledgement.

Preface

The writer’s own first page introducing the subject, work done, significance and organisation; kept short.

Foreword

A recommendation written by an expert or celebrity, not the author.

Acknowledgement

A separate page thanking those who helped, in simple polite language, in 2–3 paragraphs.

Table of contents

Division of the report into parts with page numbers.

List of illustrations

Separate list of tables, charts, graphs with numbers and pages.

Abstract

A very short condensed statement of the focus of the report, with key words.

Summary

A longer account (up to 1.5–2 pages) of the entire report.

Executive summary

The whole report in a nutshell for top managers: purpose, objectives, methodology, findings, recommendations.

Core concepts

Three parts of a technical report

Prefatory material (front matter) → Main body (text) → Back matter. Like a letter’s introduction, middle and end. Check the TOR for what the report needs; short reports need not follow the full structure. Analogy: a palatial building built brick by brick on a foundation.

Items of front matter (order)

  1. Cover
  2. Frontispiece
  3. Title page
  4. Copyright notice
  5. Forwarding letter
  6. Preface
  7. Acknowledgement
  8. Table of contents
  9. List of illustrations
  10. Abstract (or summary)

Cover page

  • Thick paper, usually white (coloured allowed); many organisations have a proforma.
  • Contents: ‘Report on’, title (key words; capitals or initial capitals except prepositions), report number (right), writer’s name (employee or enrollment number), ‘Prepared for’ (person), organisation, month and year.
  • Number and date help later researchers.

Title page

  • Gives title and subtitle (e.g. ‘Quality Health Management in Hospitals: A Case Study’).
  • Prepared for / prepared by; ‘Approved by’ at lower left with the controlling officer’s name.
  • Studies: descriptive (demand of two-wheelers), correlational, causal (effect of pollution on mankind) help in choosing titles.

Frontispiece, copyright, forwarding letter

  • Frontispiece: only bound, thick reports; protects drawings and charts; now often dropped.
  • Copyright notice: on the back of the title page with year and ‘All rights reserved’.
  • Forwarding letter: in business-letter format; certifies the report, states authorisation and scope; not mandatory.

Preface versus foreword; acknowledgement

  • Preface: by the author; foreword: by an expert as recommendation.
  • Keep preface short; put thanks on a separate page.
  • Acknowledgement: 2–3 paragraphs, categorise helpers, vary phrases (“I am thankful to…”, “I shall be failing in my duty if…”), avoid jargon and metaphor.

Table of contents and illustrations

  • Differs from outline by page numbers; title in the centre/top.
  • Order: Preface, Acknowledgement, Abstract (in italics, Roman numerals), then chapters from Introduction with Arabic numbering, then appendix and bibliography.
  • Sub-divisions are not essential.
  • List of illustrations gives number, title and page of tables and charts (graphs, pie charts) for readers who scan visuals.

Abstract, summary, executive summary

  • Abstract: very short, key words, main focus; as at conferences.
  • Summary: longer, covers the report; up to 1.5–2 pages.
  • Executive summary: whole report in a nutshell: reason for writing, objectives, findings, methodology, recommendations; topic sentences in paragraphs.
  • Language: neutral, scientific, detached, no flowery words or jargon; it prepares the reader for the report.

Summary

A technical report has front matter, main body and back matter. The front matter contains the cover, frontispiece, title page, copyright notice, forwarding letter, preface, acknowledgement, table of contents, list of illustrations and abstract or summary. The table of contents carries page numbers. Abstracts and summaries use neutral, concise language.

Lecture 37: Structure of Reports: Part-II

Week 8 · Course book PDF pages 593–608 (printed pages 592–607)

Learning objectives

  • List the four parts of the main body: introduction, discussion, conclusion, recommendation.
  • Describe the contents of an introduction.
  • Explain how to write discussion, conclusions and recommendations.
  • List the items of back matter: appendix, list of references, bibliography, glossary, index.
  • Differentiate bibliography and list of references; APA and MLA styles.

Important definitions

Introduction

First part of the main body: background, problem, authorisation, scope, limitations, sources and methods, definitions of terms and report preview.

Discussion

The part where data are analysed, interpreted and evaluated to lead logically to the conclusion.

Conclusion

Logical inferences and judgments based only on the analysis, with no new information.

Recommendation

Feasible action suggested on the basis of the conclusions, written only when asked.

Back matter

Supporting material after the main text: appendices, references, bibliography, glossary, index.

Appendix

Supporting material (questionnaire, interview sheets, charts) placed at the end; labelled Appendix A, B, C.

List of references

Only the works cited in the text; mandatory.

Bibliography

Complete alphabetical list of works consulted, including those not quoted, for further reading.

Glossary

Alphabetical list of technical terms with meanings.

Index

Alphabetical list of topics and sub-topics with page numbers, for very long reports.

APA style

Citation style common in sciences; year follows the author’s name.

MLA style

Citation style common in humanities/social sciences; year given near the end.

Core concepts

Four parts of the main body

  1. Introduction
  2. Discussion
  3. Conclusion
  4. Recommendation (if asked)

Use decimal numbering; Introduction is 1, Discussion 2 and so on.

Contents of the introduction

  • Historical background and statement of the problem (about the organisation, sector, or issue).
  • Authorisation and terms of reference; hypothesis or concept of the problem.
  • Scope – who or what is affected.
  • Limitations of the findings and methods.
  • Sources and methods of data collection.
  • Definitions of terms for non-expert readers (e.g. the boss).
  • Report preview – division of the report into sections.

Discussion

  • ‘The entire drama takes place’: analysis, interpretation and evaluation of data.
  • Divide into sections and sub-sections with decimal numbers.
  • Present findings in tables, graphs and charts that are in sync with the text; useful for busy readers.
  • Example: absenteeism – the data may show personal problems, unsuitable unit or poor communication with bosses, not mere insincerity.
  • Raise questions and answer them with data.

Conclusion

  • Based entirely on the analysis; no new information.
  • Brief (1–2 pages even for a 30-page report); may be numbered.
  • Busy readers read only conclusions and illustrations.

Recommendation

  • Give only when asked.
  • Must be feasible, not aggressive or ‘the only solution’, no personality; maintain equilibrium between truth and feasibility.
  • Infeasible or invented recommendations make the report fail.

Back matter items

  • Appendices (A, B, C) – questionnaires, interview sheets, charts that would block the flow; only interested readers consult them.
  • List of references – works cited; bibliography – all works consulted; glossary; index.
  • Few references can be footnotes on the same page; many go to the end.

Documentation styles

  • Order of entry: author (surname first in bibliography; full name in footnote), year (APA: after author), title (italic), edition, place, publisher, year (MLA: at end), pages.
  • Types: one, two, three authors, corporate author, e-mail, newspaper article, essay in an edited book, abstract.
  • Example MLA: Gibaldi, Joseph. MLA Handbook for Writers of Research Papers. New York: Prentice Hall, year.
  • Entries are alphabetical; follow your organisation’s style (italics or underline).

Glossary and index

  • Glossary: alphabetical technical terms; essential when many terms (more than 10–20).
  • Index: alphabetical; main topics and sub-topics; only for extremely long reports.

Lecture 38: Report Style: Part I

Week 8 · Course book PDF pages 609–630 (printed pages 608–629)

Learning objectives

  • Explain why report language must be familiar, specific and simple.
  • Identify and correct clichés, jargon, foreign words, redundancy, circumlocution and unfamiliar abbreviations.
  • Replace difficult words and phrasal verbs with simple ones.
  • Correct misplaced modifiers and use specific rather than vague words.
  • Recall Nathaniel Hawthorne’s remark on words.

Important definitions

Cliché

A word or phrase once fresh but now overused and stale (e.g. ‘threadbare’, ‘uphill task’, ‘writing on the wall’, ‘leave no stone unturned’).

Jargon

Technical language of a profession that non-specialist readers cannot follow.

Redundancy

Using unnecessary words that repeat meaning (basic fundamentals, repeat again, final result, great majority).

Circumlocution

A roundabout, wordy way of saying something simple.

Foreign words and phrases

Words from other languages (e.g. subpoena, avant-garde, tête-à-tête) that readers may not know.

Modifier

A word (adjective/adverb) that describes another; piled-up or misplaced modifiers confuse.

Phrasal verb / idiom

Verb plus particle (make out, make up) with changed meaning; avoid in reports.

Synonym

A word with a near meaning, used to replace a difficult word.

Abbreviation

Shortened form; use only if familiar to readers (HIV is fine; FIG, ATR, CVR may not be).

Core concepts

Why style matters

  • A report is written to bring action; action requires understanding.
  • Readers have different backgrounds (an engineer and a doctor use different terminologies).
  • Technical language is needed for technical subjects, but use words that are at least familiar.
  • Report language is not only sentences but also words and paragraphs: choose words, phrases, sentences and paragraphs carefully.
  • Hawthorne: “Words so innocent and powerless as they are, as standing in a dictionary, how potent for good and evil they become in the hands of one who knows how to combine them.”

Be specific and familiar

  • Avoid unusual words used for an ‘air of intellectuality’.
  • Be specific: ‘the majority were divided’ → ‘70 per cent were divided’.
  • Revise and replace difficult words with simple synonyms.

Difficult word replacements

  • ramifications → results; corroborate → verify / prove
  • veracity → truth; validation → proof
  • apogee → orbit (as given); camaraderie → friendship; dismember → cut; impasse → deadlock/difficulty
  • obtuse → dull; synergy → unity/coordination
  • antiquated → old; annihilation → destruction; conflagration → fire
  • cognizant → aware/know; pedagogue → teacher

Examples: “The ramifications of the experiment should be corroborated” → “The result of the lab experiment should be verified.” “The veracity of the scientist’s claim requires validation” → “The truth of the claim requires proof.”

Modifiers and phrasal verbs

  • Avoid double modifiers: “all praises and credits”, “new and novel structure” – use one word.
  • Avoid phrasal verbs: “could not make out” → “could not understand”.
  • Misplaced modifier: “Speaking before the large audience, my knees began to knock” → “Speaking before the large audience, I felt my knees knocking.”

Clichés

  • “The report discusses threadbare the problem…” → “…in detail”.
  • “uphill task” → “challenge”; “writing on the wall” → “prediction”.
  • “leave no stone unturned” → “work hard / try my best”; “burning question” → “common issue”; “day in and day out”; “repeat again”.

Foreign words, jargon, redundancy, circumlocution

  • Foreign: subpoena (court summons), avant-garde → pioneer, tête-à-tête → personal interview.
  • Jargon: “the spectrum of infection with the human immunodeficiency virus continues to evolve…” → “we are learning more about the range of infection in people with HIV”; “pedagogical pursuits” → “teaching”.
  • Redundancy: basic fundamentals, repeat again, final result, a great majority.
  • Circumlocution: “We are cognizant that you are experiencing difficulties during this period of reorganisation” → “We know…”.

Abbreviations and slang

  • Use only abbreviations the reader knows; explain unfamiliar ones (FIG = Faculty Initiation Grant; CVR = cockpit voice recorder; ATR).
  • Avoid campus slang such as ‘insti’, ‘poly class’, ‘audie’ (auditorium), ‘enthu’.
  • Take the medium path: maximum familiar words.

Wrong word choice

Confusing sensitive with sensible leads to awkward writing; the doctor cannot cure what he cannot diagnose, and a solution needs understanding. Next lecture: sentence structure, readability, active/passive.

Lecture 39: Report Style: Part II

Week 8 · Course book PDF pages 631–650 (printed pages 630–649)

Learning objectives

  • Use active and passive voice appropriately in reports.
  • Apply sentence-style techniques: subject-verb closeness, emphatic word order, parallel construction, splitting breathless sentences, avoiding noun clusters.
  • Build unified, coherent, ordered paragraphs and use transitions.
  • Compute the Gunning fog index and interpret it.
  • Recall George Orwell’s ‘windowpane’ principle.

Important definitions

Active voice

The subject performs the action; direct, clear, but can sound like an allegation.

Passive voice

The subject receives the action (verb in third form); neutral, polite, impersonal, used to avoid blame.

Emphatic word order

Arranging words so the important item is at the beginning or end of the sentence.

Parallel construction

Using the same grammatical pattern for parts of a sentence.

Breathless sentence

A long sentence with no pause; split it into two or more.

Noun cluster (noun string)

A long chain of nouns (e.g. ‘current group member meeting dates scheduling package format’) that hides meaning; replace with verbs and prepositions.

Transitions (signposts)

Linking words and phrases that guide readers from one idea to the next.

Readability

The ease with which a text can be read and understood.

Fog index

Robert Gunning’s formula: 0.4 × (average sentence length + percentage of difficult words); a score below 12 means readable.

Topic sentence

The main sentence of a paragraph to which others are related.

Core concepts

Variety as a style principle

  • Repeating the same pattern (‘I am thankful to…’ repeatedly) is monotonous; vary voice, sentence length and order.
  • Mix short and long sentences; give paragraphs for ‘eye relief’.
  • Examples of variety in acknowledgement: ‘I am indebted to…’, ‘I acknowledge…’.

Active versus passive voice

  • Active: direct and easier (“We do not advise the training division to concentrate its time, money and resources on a new programme”).
  • Passive: “Concentration… is not advisable from our point of view.”
  • Use passive to neutralise blame: “Since your workers did not pack the tape recorders, they got damaged” (allegation) → “Since the tape recorders were not packed properly…”.
  • Commands (‘Shut the door’) can be softened: “You are requested to close the door.”
  • “You are responsible” → “I think it is your responsibility.”
  • A complaint is written in active voice; the reply is often in passive.

Sentence style

  • Put the subject first and the verb just after it; don’t let the verb get lost at the end.
  • Place important matter in the subject; use emphatic word order.
  • Revise: “Some aspects of desktop publishing… have powerful control…” → “Today even a low-cost word processor can deliver…”.
  • Link clauses: “Parts are manufactured in Mexico, which has lower wage rates than the United States.”
  • Word order example: “He, as a management representative, is aware that he should uphold the authority to fire workers who disobey orders.”
  • Put the positive first: “We feel that by using this system we are missing some patients and therefore losing revenue.”

Parallelism and splitting

  • Parallel: “This book is good as a college text and even better as a means of enlarging their knowledge.”
  • Split breathless sentences: “We are enclosing next month’s event calendar. If interested, kindly inform the concerned organiser.”
  • Complex scientific sentences (adrenergic–cholinergic antagonism) become readable when split and using pronouns.

Noun clusters

Kick the noun habit, create the verb habit: “Please revise the schedule package format of group members’ meetings” instead of a string of nouns. Value-orientation example: bring the verb closer (“The concept of value orientation in this sense is thus…”).

Paragraph structure

  • Three mantras: unity, coherence, order.
  • Begin with a topic sentence; other sentences coordinate or subordinate to it using connecting words, repetition and pronouns.
  • Develop paragraphs by comparison and contrast, cause and effect, classification, problem–solution.
  • Avoid very long paragraphs; paragraph breaks give relief.

Transitions

  • Addition: moreover, furthermore, besides, finally.
  • Cause / result: hence, therefore, thus, consequently.
  • Contrast: however, nonetheless, on the other hand, while.
  • Time: earlier, now, later, after a while, meanwhile.
  • Repetition / summary: in short, that is, thus.

Readability and fog index

  • Gunning (1950s) fog index = 0.4 × (average sentence length + percentage of difficult words).
  • Result below 12 = readable.
  • Orwell: style should be unobtrusive – like a windowpane through which information is clearly seen.

Lecture 40: Group Communication

Week 8 · Course book PDF pages 651–668 (printed pages 650–667)

Learning objectives

  • Define a group and a team and differentiate them.
  • List the tasks of groups and types of groups.
  • State the advantages and disadvantages of group work, including groupthink and hidden agendas.
  • Explain group dynamics: cooperation, loyalty, cohesiveness.
  • Describe Tuckman’s stages of group development and the three group roles.

Important definitions

Group

A collection of people who work, interact and cooperate to achieve a common goal in a specified time; identity of members is taken individually and they share information and resources.

Team

A group of people with a common goal, within a limited time, having collective accountability; slogan: ‘one for all and all for one’.

Formal group

A group formally constituted by an organisation.

Informal group

A group formed through personal relations; often works better in problem solving.

Virtual group

A group communicating online (WhatsApp, Facebook).

Groupthink

A poor group decision resulting from majority pressure that ignores individual dissent.

Hidden agenda

A concealed personal aim of a member that harms group interaction.

Group dynamics

Forces that keep a group working: cooperation, loyalty, cohesiveness.

Cohesiveness

Staying together and committed to the goal despite disagreements.

Consensus

General agreement reached by the group.

Core concepts

Why groups

  • Humans form groups; modern tasks (events, reports, products, presentations) cannot be done alone or in short time.
  • Example: a college cultural festival (‘Cognizance’) is a group effort; teams for music, lectures etc.
  • This lecture itself is a team product (recording, editing, approving, posting).

Group versus team

  • Group: common goal within a period; members taken individually.
  • Team: a subdivision with its own specific goal and collective accountability; shared leadership; joint accountability for performance.
  • Both share a common goal, cooperation, identification of skills, leadership and accountability.

Tasks of groups and teams

  1. Solve problems.
  2. Investigate an opportunity (new product, policy).
  3. Write reports.
  4. Give oral or group presentations (experts present their parts).
  5. Devise, evolve and produce new products (e.g. incubation centres).

Types of groups

  • Formal, informal (often more effective) and virtual groups.
  • Informal groups help problem solving: members help each other beyond formal roles.

Advantages of groups

  • More information and resources.
  • Diversity of views and experience.
  • Better acceptance of decisions and consensus after weighing pros and cons.
  • Better performance.
  • Sense of belonging; broadened knowledge.

Disadvantages of groups

  • Rigid ideas and ego clashes cause wasted, frustrating time.
  • ‘Know-it-all’ delusion; unacceptance of consensus.
  • Groups within groups; individual opinions ignored.
  • Groupthink and poor decisions.
  • Hidden agendas.
  • Difficulty coordinating diverse backgrounds; a ‘messiah’/leader is needed.

Group dynamics

  • Cooperation – communication clears roadblocks.
  • Loyalty – willingness to make small sacrifices.
  • Cohesiveness – staying together despite differences (utensils make noise).

Tuckman’s stages

  1. Forming – introductions, rapport building; members are new.
  2. Storming – clash of ideas; beneficial for new ideas.
  3. Norming – peace; members mellow, accept views, filter.
  4. Performing – energetic, effective work.
  5. Adjourning – group dissolves after the task (added later by Tuckman).

Bruce Tuckman, psychologist; initially four stages, adjourning added later.

Group roles

  • Self-oriented roles – members pursuing self-interest, wanting control or withdrawing.
  • Maintenance roles – encouraging, harmonising, compromising to keep the group together.
  • Task-facilitating roles – initiating, giving and seeking information, setting procedures.

Leader and Drucker

A leader coordinates and secures consensus. Peter Drucker: “When the ship goes down, you do not call a meeting. The captain gives an order or everybody drowns.” (crisis needs decisive leadership). Members must also consider ethics and personal responsibility. Next lecture: leadership.
