Lecture 31: Report Writing

Week 7 · Course book PDF pages 504–517 (printed pages 503–516)

Learning objectives

  • State the origin and definition of a report.
  • List the attributes of a report: formal, factual, specific purpose and audience, organised, unified, illustrated, bound.
  • Explain the objectives of reports.
  • Differentiate report writing from literary writing.

Important definitions

Report

A formal communication written for a specific purpose and audience; it describes the procedures followed for collecting and analysing data and gives conclusions and, if required, recommendations.

Etymology of report

From Latin reportare = re (back) + portare (to carry): to carry information to a person who is not present. (The transcript glosses ‘re’ as ‘to send’.)

Unity and coherence

Every sentence and paragraph links to the topic and to one another, giving a smooth flow of thought.

Cross-fertilisation of ideas

Exchange of ideas across people and departments through reports.

Core concepts

Why reports matter

  • Decisions (promotion, product launch, new policy) rest on reports; a committee studies data and concludes.
  • Doctor’s medical tests are an everyday analogy of procedure, data, interpretation and conclusion.
  • A report is the “bread and butter” of an organisation; it can make or mar the fortune of both organisation and individual.

Attributes of a report

  1. Formal piece of writing.
  2. Factual – data-based, objective, no wrong data.
  3. Written for a specific audience (language adapted; computer professional versus layman).
  4. Written for a specific purpose; only when required (need-based).
  5. Systematic and well organised (beginning, discussion, end).
  6. Unity and coherence.
  7. Uses illustrations (charts, graphs, pie charts, tables).
  8. Bound properly with a cover page and title; sequence of parts.

Objectives of a report

  • Update progress of the organisation.
  • Evaluate performance (e.g. five-year data and trends).
  • Guide the organisation to a corrective path when things go wrong (committee, data, conclusions).
  • Spread information internally and externally to stakeholders and shareholders.
  • Cross-fertilise ideas and reveal gaps in reasoning.
  • Record findings and recommend action.
  • Create awareness and self-awareness (compare yourself with a promoted colleague).

Professional’s only tangible product

A report is “a professional’s only tangible product”: it often is the only contact between an engineer/professional and management, so it shows reasoning, scientific attitude, grasp, intelligence and initiative; clarity is the hallmark.

Lecture 32: Types of Reports

Week 7 · Course book PDF pages 518–528 (printed pages 517–527)

Learning objectives

  • Classify reports by length, form, nature, frequency and purpose.
  • Differentiate short (informal) and technical (formal) reports.
  • Differentiate informative and analytical (interpretive) reports.
  • Explain routine, management and special reports.
  • Recall the bathing-suit quotation by Sherring and Christian.

Important definitions

Short report

An informal, brief report (one page to about 10 pages) with an authorisation, a problem, a conclusion and, only if asked, a recommendation; includes letter reports and memo reports.

Technical report

A formal, documented report with prefatory material, deep and systematic discussion, data analysis, illustrations and back matter.

Informative report

A report that presents information as it is, without analysis or recommendation (e.g. inspection, lab, attendance reports).

Analytical (interpretive) report

A report that interprets, analyses and evaluates data, reaches conclusions and may recommend action.

Routine report

A report written regularly at fixed intervals on a fixed proforma, dealing with regular matters (finance, production, sales, inventory).

Management report

A non-technical, short report for top managers, with restricted language, journalistic tone and more illustrations.

Special report

A report written during a critical juncture or aberration, with no standard format and time pressure.

ACR

Annual Confidential Report: a routine report evaluating an employee’s performance.

Appendix

Back matter where supporting material that would block the flow of the report is placed.

Core concepts

Ways of classifying reports

  • By length: short and long.
  • By form: written and oral.
  • By subject / nature: management and technical.
  • By frequency: routine and special.
  • By purpose / function: informative and analytical (interpretive).

Short reports

  • Informal; e.g. a school picnic report; one page to 5–6 or 8–10 pages.
  • Needs a letter of authorisation and a statement of the problem; then conclusion.
  • Recommendation only if asked: conclusions rest on data, recommendations bring in your own thoughts.
  • Letter reports (salutation, complimentary close, informal tone) and memo reports (no salutation or close) are short reports.
  • Be concise, plain, direct; ask ‘why am I writing this?’; review before sending; move blocking material to the appendix.

Technical reports

  • Proper documentation: beginning, prefatory material, discussion, analysis, illustrations, supporting material.
  • Very detailed, logical, systematic discussion to analyse a problem and provide a solution.
  • Data must be factual and its sources authentic: wrong data leads to wrong decisions that harm the institution.
  • Data is collected, analysed, interpreted and sampled to reach a conclusion (and recommendation, if required).
  • Analysis is divided into sections and subsections (e.g. pollution, garbage disposal) and must lead logically to the conclusion.
  • Supporting material (sources, methods) goes to the back matter.
  • Divided into informative and analytical.

Informative reports

  • Simply convey information as it is, not as it should be; no analysis, usually shorter.
  • Often a fixed proforma with ticks; little language use; the writer does not recommend action.
  • Examples: market survey, laboratory report, inventory, inspection, traffic observation.
  • Also: periodic (weekly attendance, monthly inspection), readership surveys, employee booklets, prospectus, website information, committee reports, policy statements, sales, credit and production reports.

Analytical (interpretive) reports

  • Interpret, analyse and evaluate data; investigate a problem (e.g. a product with deficiencies) and may recommend action.
  • Writer must be unbiased and keep personal bias out.
  • Elaborate and descriptive: may run 30–35 pages.
  • Example: a Pay Commission examines situations, recommends, and government decides.
  • More objective and scientific; analysis must serve the common good.
  • Busy readers read the illustrations (tables, charts, graphs), so use relevant visuals.

Routine reports

  • Regular, at fixed intervals (fortnight, month, year); fixed proforma; little language.
  • Deal with finance, production, sales, performance, inventory; keep internal people and outside agencies informed.
  • Examples: annual reports, project progress proforma (every six months), lab report, stock verification, inspection reports (e.g. car makers withdrawing or adding features), ACR (annual confidential report).

Management reports

  • Non-technical, short, for top people; more illustrations, little explanation.
  • Language restricted, tone at times journalistic.
  • Managers read the conclusion and may refer to the technical report for detail.
  • Writer must know the readers’ background, age, taste and preferences.

Special reports

  • Written only in critical hours or after a mishap; low frequency; time limit.
  • No standard format; writer relies on caution and felicity of language.
  • Writer must be extra vigilant, diligent, intelligent and careful because action will follow.

Key quotation

Sherring and Christian: “A report is like a bathing suit; it covers everything that has to be covered, but nothing more.” (No unnecessary or irrelevant material.)

Lecture 33: Strategies for Report Writing Part I

Week 7 · Course book PDF pages 529–545 (printed pages 528–544)

Learning objectives

  • List the steps in preparing to write a report: purpose and scope, audience, data collection, organising material, outline.
  • Explain terms of reference (TOR) and why knowing the reader matters.
  • Distinguish primary and secondary sources of data.
  • Evaluate personal observation: process, precautions, limitations.
  • State the advantages and disadvantages of telephonic interviews.

Important definitions

Terms of reference (TOR)

Instructions from the person assigning the report stating the problem, scope, readers and what is to be done.

Scope

The extent of the problem the report covers and who will read it.

Primary source

First-hand data from personal observation and investigation.

Secondary source

Existing data used to verify or supplement: library, Internet, internal records.

Personal observation

Seeing with a purpose; the word is explained in the lecture as ob + servation.

Controlled observation

Observation where the subject knows or is prepared in advance.

Uncontrolled observation

Observation where the subject is unaware of being observed.

Primary reader

The boss or person who assigned the report.

Secondary reader

Other readers, including those outside the organisation with relevant expertise.

Telephonic interview

Collecting data by planned telephone questioning.

Core concepts

Steps before writing a report

  1. Identify purpose and scope.
  2. Determine the audience.
  3. Choose the right method of data collection.
  4. Organise the material.
  5. Make an outline.

Purpose, scope and TOR

  • Ask: why am I writing, and who gave me the task?
  • The assigner supplies the terms of reference: problem, scope, readers, what to cover.
  • Example: Mess Secretary asked by the Chief Warden to report on mess functioning (timing, food quality, vegetarian meals, sweets).

Knowing the audience

  • Readers: superior officers, colleagues, counterparts, subordinates, people of other organisations, shareholders, customers, the public. (In the example: Chief Warden, Dean Students’ Welfare, Deputy Director, Director.)
  • Know their background, your relationship with them and their attitude (favourable or hostile); this guides language and presentation.
  • Primary reader = boss; secondary readers = others, including outsiders with expertise.
  • Do not be influenced by the assigner’s expectations: work on the data to stay unbiased.

Sources of data

  • Primary: personal observation and investigation (verified later).
  • Secondary: library, Internet, internal records.
  • Ways of collection: personal observation, telephonic interviews, personal interviews, questionnaires.
  • Use specific words in search engines; avoid straying to irrelevant sites; verify authenticity with other sources without wasting time.

Personal observation: how to do it

  • Fix beforehand what data you want (a habit, trend, quality).
  • Decide between controlled and uncontrolled observation; people change behaviour when they know they are watched.
  • Do not display pen and paper; note afterwards from memory when observing people (open note-taking is fine for traffic counts).
  • Ignore sudden irrelevant changes (posture, gesture); stay focused.
  • Avoid prejudice and see with the ‘mind’s eye’; first impressions (e.g. a tired-looking speaker) may be wrong.
  • Covert recording that is later disclosed creates problems; be specific, not general.

Limitations of observation

  • Cannot be the only method; gives first-hand but not necessarily representative data.
  • Can report behaviour but not interpret it.
  • Biased by the observer; one day may not represent others (e.g. rainy Sunday traffic).
  • Slow, costly, and subjects may be unavailable.

Telephonic interviews

Advantages: accessible anywhere, saves time and travel, wide geographic reach, low refusal rate.

Disadvantages: needs meticulous planning and specific questions; casual talk differs from data-collecting talk; detailed data is hard to get; no sight of non-verbal cues (video calls help only partly); cannot judge age or nationality or if the respondent hides information; limited time, so follow-up is hard; respondents may give wrong information or send someone else.

Lecture 34: Strategies for Report Writing Part II

Week 7 · Course book PDF pages 546–560 (printed pages 545–559)

Learning objectives

  • Define a personal interview and list its advantages and precautions.
  • Explain questionnaires, their advantages and disadvantages.
  • List the types of questions: closed, rating, open-ended, multiple choice.
  • Apply guidelines for framing a questionnaire and covering letter.
  • Recall Freud’s and Ruskin’s quotations.

Important definitions

Personal interview

A process of seeking information directly through a face-to-face conversation with a purpose.

Questionnaire

A set of standardised questions sent to many respondents to collect data without travel.

Closed question

A question with a fixed answer such as yes/no (e.g. ‘Do you smoke?’).

Rating question

A question asking the respondent to rate on a scale such as 1 to 10.

Open-ended question

A question allowing respondents to express their own opinion in their own words.

Multiple-choice question

A question with (typically four) offered answers.

Leading question

A question that suggests the expected answer or is embarrassing or tricky.

Covering letter

A letter accompanying a questionnaire explaining its purpose and assuring confidentiality.

Representative sample

A group of respondents whose answers reflect the whole population.

Core concepts

Why other methods are needed

Observation and telephonic interviews have limits and their data may not be representative, so data should be verified through personal interviews and questionnaires.

Personal interview: advantages

  • Face-to-face communication is better because non-words (dress, bearing, gestures, postures, silence, pauses) are available.
  • Gathers attitudes and feelings; faces reveal emotions.
  • Learns from people with intimate knowledge of the problem; verifies and supplements observation, questionnaire or telephone data.
  • The interviewer has control and can bring the interviewee back to the point.
  • Questions can be interpreted and ambiguities cleared; replies are more representative (people may lie on the phone but less so in person).
  • Gives first-hand impressions and immediate feedback.

Freud: “He who has eyes to see and ears to hear can convince himself that no mortal can keep a secret. If his lips are silent, he chatters with his fingertips; betrayal oozes out of him at every pore.”

Personal interview: tips and disadvantages

  • Costly and needs travel.
  • Seek an appointment in advance, state the purpose; be courteous and tactful.
  • Standardise questions; limit time (busy professionals).
  • Jot notes if allowed; recording is possible.
  • Do not argue; give the interviewee room; speak less, listen carefully; take a subordinate role.
  • Avoid embarrassing questions that affect the interviewee’s image; frame questions subtly.

Questionnaire: advantages

  • Saves travel, time and money; reaches people in different locations with one set of questions.
  • Convenient for respondents, who can look up information at leisure.
  • No interviewer bias; respondents are fearless and may stay anonymous.
  • Standardised questions; follow-up questions can follow closed ones.
  • Return postage can be prepaid.

Questionnaire: disadvantages

  • Low response; people decide on likes and dislikes, so the sample may not be representative.
  • Uncertainty; questions left unanswered or answered inadequately or meaninglessly.
  • Reluctance on confidential or complex matters.
  • No control over the situation.
  • Cost per mailing is low but cost per return is high.

Framing a questionnaire

  • Keep it short, specific and attractive; too long means unanswered.
  • Two parts: personal information first, then the main questions.
  • Use simple wording; arrange logically with coherence.
  • Place tricky questions in the middle, not at the beginning or the end.
  • Avoid leading and embarrassing questions (e.g. about alcohol brand, newspaper read).
  • Be free of grammatical errors.
  • Include a covering letter stating purpose and assuring confidentiality.

Ask direct questions: ‘Do you smoke?’ rather than ‘What is your reaction to smoking?’.

Closing quotation

John Ruskin: “To be able to ask a question clearly is two-thirds of the way to getting it answered.”

After collection: analyse, organise, outline, then write.

Summary

Personal interviews give reliable, verifiable, first-hand data with non-verbal cues and interviewer control but are costly; the interviewer should plan, listen, avoid arguments and embarrassing questions. Questionnaires reach many respondents cheaply and anonymously but risk low and unrepresentative responses. A good questionnaire is short, simple, logical, error-free, uses closed, rating, open-ended and multiple-choice questions, avoids leading questions, places tricky ones in the middle and carries a covering letter. Ruskin: a clearly asked question is two-thirds answered.

Lecture 35: Evaluation and Organization of Data

Week 7 · Course book PDF pages 561–575 (printed pages 560–574)

Learning objectives

  • Explain why collected data must be evaluated and organised.
  • Describe probability and non-probability sampling and their types.
  • Apply note-making techniques.
  • State the principles of an outline: indenting, numbering, coordination, subordination, parallel construction, logical ordering.
  • Prepare a decimal-numbered outline.

Important definitions

Sampling

Selecting a representative part of the data so that conclusions can be drawn for the whole.

Probability sampling

Sampling in which every item has a known chance of selection: random, systematic or stratified.

Random sampling

Selecting items purely by chance so that every item has an equal chance; the ‘purest’ way.

Systematic sampling

Choosing records by a fixed system, e.g. every ninth record.

Stratified sampling

Dividing the data into strata (e.g. men/women, students/teachers, children/adults) and sampling from each.

Non-probability sampling

Sampling by convenience or judgment of the analyst.

Outline

A rough, tentative, short structure of the whole report; a distribution table showing which information goes to which section.

Coordination

Main head and sub-heads of the same level are of equal importance and belong to the same subject.

Subordination

Sub-heads are less important than, and visibly lower than, the main head (indent and typeface).

Parallel grammatical construction

Headings of the same level use the same grammatical form (all nouns, all phrases).

Note cards

Cards with sections and sub-sections used to sort jumbled data.

Core concepts

From data to report

Collected data is huge and haphazard. Steps: evaluate (validate, sample) → make notes → organise → outline → write.

Sampling methods

  • Probability: random, systematic (e.g. every ninth record), stratified (groups by criteria).
  • Non-probability: convenience, judgment.
  • Aim: representative data so the report can lead to a solution.

Making notes

  • Notes later create the outline; report writing is a systematic process.
  • Place quoted words in quotation marks and give credit.
  • Add a small summary; use abbreviations (shorthand is abandoned now).
  • Use note cards with sections and sub-sections.
  • Write clearly so you can read your own notes.
  • Record observations, facts and trends under suitable categories.

Principles of outline

  1. Indenting – sub-topics shifted to the right, main topics at left.
  2. Numbering – Roman, alphabetical or decimal (preferred: 1, 2, 3.1, 3.2.1); do not go beyond the third level.
  3. Coordination – no new matter unrelated to the head.
  4. Subordination – sub-heads visibly less important (bold head, normal sub-heads).
  5. Parallel grammatical construction – all nouns/phrases/adjectives, not a mix.
  6. Logical ordering – a sequence such as introduction, history, discussion, conclusion, recommendation, bibliography; stick to the pattern chosen.
  7. Phrasing – use phrases, not full sentences.

Sample outlines

Advertising in India: 1 Introduction; 2 History; 3 Types → 3.1 Print, 3.2 Electronic → 3.2.1 (sub-type).

Modes of presentation: reading from manuscript, impromptu, memorisation (coordinated, no new matter).

Benefits of advertising: economic, political, moral, religious, cultural (all adjectives; not ‘politics’ or ‘moralise’).

Advantages of nationalisation: mobilisation of national resources, promotion of agricultural production… (nouns, not ‘to promote’).

Role of the outline

  • A tailor’s analogy: cut cloth into pieces, then stitch into one whole.
  • Outline is a tentative guide that reminds you what is missing or misplaced; the writer may change it.
  • “Once the outline is ready, half the job is done.”
  • Next: structure (anatomy) of the report.
