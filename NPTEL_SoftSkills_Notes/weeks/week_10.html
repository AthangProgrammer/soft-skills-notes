Lecture 46: Advanced Speaking Skills

Week 10 · Course book PDF pages 750–767 (printed pages 749–766)

Learning objectives

  • Explain why speaking is a skill and how it differs from writing.
  • List the situations where speaking is required.
  • State the advantages of speaking over writing.
  • Describe the four components of effective speaking: voice, vocabulary, audience, style.
  • Recall the quotations by Shakespeare and Benjamin Franklin.

Important definitions

LSRW

Listening, Speaking, Reading, Writing: the four language skills; this lecture covers ‘S’.

Speaking skill

The ability to perform tasks (convince, inform, report, entertain) by spoken words coupled with non-verbal communication.

Conversation

An art of informal two-way speaking, e.g. breaking the ice with a co-passenger.

Spontaneity

Natural, unplanned quality of speech that keeps the audience engaged.

Style (in speaking)

The speaker’s inner voice: animation, enthusiasm and audibility.

Audibility

Being heard clearly even by the last row.

Core concepts

Why speaking is a skill

  • Speaking is natural, but in organisations tasks are communication-based; a professional must master all LSRW skills.
  • Individually you must convince, prove, satisfy, report, inform and entertain (as employee, manager or CEO).
  • A silent person in a group is seen as uninterested or without content.
  • Verbal communication is coupled with non-verbal cues.

Situations requiring speaking

  • Conversation, meetings, negotiations, group discussions, debates.
  • Speeches and presentations; giving or taking interviews.
  • Oral presentations; talks with several people for an MOU.

Advantages of speaking over writing

  • Face to face: immediate feedback and reading the listener’s reaction.
  • Allows clarification, dialogue and persuasion.
  • Builds credibility and rapport; more energising than reading.
  • Emotion conveyed through voice (commentary on cricket versus silent TV).
  • Clearer, more lucid, explanatory and spontaneous; reliable because of expressions.
  • Saves time; quick response; flexible tone and tenor (anger, sadness, joy, favour, instruction).
  • Voice can be modulated (also can be manipulated, as actors do).

Four components of effective speaking

  1. Voice – God’s gift but the way of speaking can be trained (singers train pitch); aim at clarity; avoid too fast (may signal fear) or too slow.
  2. Vocabulary – enriched over years; must use right words in the right place; words are context-based (e.g. ‘recycle’ for a computer person versus a pharmacist); use familiar words.
  3. Audience – know age, background, taste, gender and education; if unknown, imagine a mixed gathering; size affects enthusiasm.
  4. Style – enthusiasm and animation; audibility to the last row (check microphone); a vibrant start and varied tone, never monotone.

Quotations

  • “Half the world is full of people who have nothing to say and keep saying on; the other half has too much to say yet cannot say it.”
  • Shakespeare: men of few words are the best men.
  • Benjamin Franklin: “Remember not only to say the right thing in the right place, but far more difficult still, to leave unsaid the wrong thing at the tempting moment.”
  • Every speaker wins or loses in the beginning.

Lecture 47: Oral Presentations, Speeches, and Debates

Week 10 · Course book PDF pages 768–783 (printed pages 767–782)

Learning objectives

  • Define oral presentation, speech and debate and differentiate them.
  • List occasions for oral presentations.
  • Explain the role of visual aids and the question–answer session.
  • Describe the purposes and features of speeches.
  • State how to prepare for and deliver a debate, including rebuttal and courtesy (Roosevelt).

Important definitions

Oral presentation

A planned, factual, professional talk to a small, defined audience to inform or persuade, usually with visual aids and a question–answer session leading to a decision.

Speech

Formal face-to-face oral communication to an audience for a purpose, relying on voice, vocabulary, gesture and posture, usually without visual aids.

Debate

A formal contest of arguments on a motion, with speakers for or against, requiring research, logic and rebuttal.

Visual aids

Charts, graphs, tables, film clips and live demonstrations used in presentations.

Briefing

A short presentation to update a group (press, vendors, senior executives).

Rebuttal

The time to counter the opponent’s arguments.

Orientation programme

A presentation introducing new members to an organisation or course.

Core concepts

Purposes of speaking

All speaking situations aim to inform, persuade or entertain.

Oral presentation

  • Given to a small defined group; more challenging because they listen to every word.
  • Factual and not too emotional; informative or persuasive.
  • Uses visual aids – charts, graphs, tables, film clips, live demo – to satisfy curiosity.
  • Leads to a decision (e.g. a paperless office, biometric attendance).
  • Question–answer session: anticipate questions and prepare answers; convince the audience.

Occasions for presentations

  • Budget presentation for a department/unit.
  • Briefing: the press, vendors, senior executives (progress updates).
  • Training and orientation of new employees, students, teachers.
  • Recruitment tips by placement officers.
  • Launching a new product and competing with others.

Speech

  • Occasions: degree ceremonies, Bhavan day, farewell, impromptu announcements; may inform, persuade or entertain.
  • Fear comes from unfamiliar crowds and lack of preparation.
  • Uses voice, vocabulary, style, gesture and posture.
  • Difference from presentation: speeches generally have no visual aids and the audience is usually larger.

Debate: value and skills

  • Builds argumentative ability and critical thinking (analysis, evaluation, conceptualisation).
  • Two sides: for or against the motion; opinion must be backed by logic and data.
  • Enriches speaking and arguing skills; topics may be literal, psychological, quotations, etc.

Preparing for a debate

  1. Research the topic (not only Google) and collect data.
  2. Draft the speech with conviction; use arguments supported by facts.
  3. Rehearse several times with a guide.
  4. Listen carefully to the opposition and prepare to counter.
  5. Use note cards that are legible.
  6. Use rebuttal time well – it can change the result.

On the day of the debate

  • Reach the venue well in time; be mentally, physically and psychologically ready.
  • Treat opponents with courtesy; greet panelists.
  • Use appropriate non-verbal behaviour; polite, harmless language even when contradicting.
  • Roosevelt: “Courtesy is as much a mark of a gentleman as courage.”

Lecture 48: Combating Nervousness

Week 10 · Course book PDF pages 784–799 (printed pages 783–798)

Learning objectives

  • Define nervousness and describe its physical and psychological symptoms.
  • Explain why nervousness is natural, universal and useful.
  • Recall the views of William James, Sartre, Dale Carnegie and Shakespeare.
  • List causes of nervousness and their remedies.
  • Apply stress busters during a talk.

Important definitions

Nervousness

A psychological and physiological state of anxiety/fear about performance, with cognitive, emotional and physical components.

Arousal

The keyed-up state caused by adrenaline surge in the body.

Adrenaline surge

Release of adrenaline that speeds the heartbeat and breathing and pumps extra energy.

Fight or flight

Psychologists’ term for the response to threat; the speaker should ‘fight’ nervousness.

Stress busters

Techniques such as deep breathing, pausing and speaking slowly to control nerves.

Rapport

Friendly connection built with the audience.

Core concepts

Symptoms

  • Butterflies and a sinking feeling in the stomach.
  • Dry throat, trembling hands, quivering voice.
  • Rapid heartbeat and fast speech; forgetting lines.
  • Feeling is mostly internal; the audience rarely notices.

Why people feel nervous

  • Fear of not performing well; concern about image before strangers.
  • Unfamiliar audience, unfamiliar or difficult topic, poor preparation, inexperience.
  • Survey cited: 80 per cent of people prefer death to public speaking.
  • Common to speakers, musicians, singers, cricketers, swimmers.

Nervousness is natural and useful

  • Natural and universal – even celebrities and experienced speakers feel it.
  • You are more conscious because you want to perform better.
  • William James: survival devices prepare us for an emergency – muscles tense for agility, heartbeat and breathing quicken, glands sharpen senses and give emergency energy.
  • It is energising and improves the talk; fears pass after the opening.
  • Analogy: a new Test batsman’s nerves vanish after the first ball; crossing a bridge carefully.
  • Sartre: people have a choice whether to be cowards.
  • Dale Carnegie: “I am OK, you are OK.” The audience will not attack you; worst case, the talk is not appreciated and you learn.
  • Shakespeare: “Cowards die many times before their death; the valiant taste of death but once.”

Remedies before the talk

  • Prepare well in advance – the best antidote or panacea.
  • Know the audience (age, education) and use examples from their field.
  • Begin with familiar topics; research thoroughly.
  • Practise before friends in small groups and take feedback.
  • Record yourself on a cell phone (audio/video) to review body language, pauses and accent.
  • Check the venue – acoustics, microphone, seating.
  • Treat the audience as friends; see their smiles and whispers as compliments.

Stress busters during the talk

  • Face the crowd: stand, pause and build rapport before the first word.
  • Take a deep breath; start with a pause, not a verbal firework.
  • Speak slowly and distinctly; experienced speakers keep a steady pace (slow and steady wins the race).
  • If you forget, review the last point; the next thought will come.
  • Concentrate on the topic, not on shaking hands; a dais hides trembling.
  • Tell yourself: ‘bear it, not fear it’.
  • Fear is in the mind; practise; learn from failures and imitate good speakers.

Lecture 49: Patterns and Methods of Presentation

Week 10 · Course book PDF pages 800–813 (printed pages 799–812)

Learning objectives

  • List the 5 Ps of presentations and the three stages of a presentation.
  • Describe the five patterns: chronological, causal, problem–solution, spatial, topical.
  • Describe the four methods of delivery: manuscript, memorised, impromptu, extemporaneous.
  • Evaluate the advantages and limitations of each method.
  • Recall the Shakespeare quotations used.

Important definitions

5 Ps

Preparation, Planning, Practice, Pattern, Performance.

Chronological pattern

Arranges material in time sequence, e.g. history of civilisation.

Causal pattern

Presents causes first and then effects (cause–effect relationship); informative.

Problem–solution pattern

Presents a problem (e.g. deforestation, pollution, global warming) and then offers solutions; persuasive.

Spatial pattern

Arranged by space or direction, e.g. design of a new product.

Topical pattern

Divides information into topics and sub-topics; easiest when others do not fit.

Reading from manuscript

Delivering a written text word for word.

Memorised method

Delivering a fully memorised text.

Impromptu method

Unprepared speech given on the spur of the moment.

Extemporaneous method

Flexible, prepared with notes/outline/slides, delivered conversationally.

Core concepts

5 Ps and stages

  • 5 Ps: preparation, planning, practice, pattern, performance.
  • Three stages: pre-presentation, during presentation, post-presentation.
  • Preparation is the best antidote to nervousness (fight or flight).

Five patterns of presentation

  1. Chronological – time sequence, historical events.
  2. Causal – causes then effects (e.g. causes and effects of nervousness).
  3. Problem–solution – problem then solution; persuasive.
  4. Spatial – space or direction, product design.
  5. Topical – topics and sub-topics; easiest.

Choose according to topic and information.

Reading from manuscript

  • Advantages: accuracy, suitable for technical or data-heavy subjects, carefully drafted words.
  • Limitations: no eye contact or rapport; no control over crowd; hard to be spontaneous; strain on eyes; little vocal variety (modulation, pauses); time limit pressure (“Damocles’ sword”); needs a practised reader; audience loses interest.
  • Not advisable for beginners.

Memorised method

  • Looks fresh and allows eye contact.
  • Problems: cannot memorise pauses, shifts in voice or transitions; speed and time control (may finish early); sounds like a robot; forgetting a part halts the talk; hard to prepare and sustain.

Impromptu method

  • No preparation: on the spot (name announced suddenly).
  • Challenging but rewarding; relies on past reading, experience and observations.
  • Be brief and to the point; take a moment, pick a keyword; summarise when ideas run out; resist the temptation to add; silence is better than saying nothing.
  • Be mentally prepared always.
  • Shakespeare: ‘It is best to speak few than to speak more’; ‘There is nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so.’

Extemporaneous method

  • Prepared flexibly: prepare more material than needed; use notes, slides and visual aids; outline ready so you can skip.
  • Conversational and interactive; gauge the audience’s mood and cut or squeeze points.
  • Needs expertise.
  • A speech may sound conversational but is not a conversation.

Summary

Presentations are built on the 5 Ps and go through pre-, during and post-presentation stages. They may follow a chronological, causal, problem–solution, spatial or topical pattern. Lecture 50: Oral Presentation: Planning & Preparation

Week 10 · Course book PDF pages 814–831 (printed pages 813–830)

Learning objectives

  • Plan an oral presentation: purpose, topic, audience analysis, research, specific purpose statement.
  • Prepare content that is audience-specific and written to be spoken.
  • Structure the presentation into introduction, body and conclusion with time proportions.
  • Plan visual aids, repetition, signposts and anticipated questions.
  • Choose topics through an inventory and brainstorming.

Important definitions

Purpose statement

A specific statement of what the talk aims to do: add knowledge, persuade, inform of trends.

Audience analysis

Studying the age, background, education, faith, gender and interests of the listeners.

Audience-specific content

Content designed to benefit the particular audience.

Verbatim

Word for word, as in a full manuscript.

Inventory of topics

A personal record of topics and new information kept for future talks.

Signposts / transfer stations

Transitions and previews that guide the audience through the talk.

Call for action

A closing appeal in problem–solution talks urging the audience to act.

Core concepts

Planning sequence

  1. Know the purpose: why, whom and how.
  2. Decide the topic (given by host or chosen by you).
  3. Analyse the audience.
  4. Research and gather information.
  5. Formulate a specific purpose statement.
  6. Prepare content and visual aids.
  7. Divide into beginning, middle, end.
  8. Plan for questions and practise.

Choosing the topic

  • Pick a topic familiar to you (reduces nervousness) but add something new for the audience.
  • Keep an inventory of topics of interest from school/college days; update it continuously (library, internet).
  • Brainstorm about the audience’s tastes, habits, interests, beliefs.
  • Mix the new and the old: familiarity increases receptivity.

Audience analysis

  • Many good presentations fail because the speaker did not understand the audience.
  • Consider age, background, education, faith, gender, rural/urban, profession; mixed gatherings are common.
  • Make the talk beneficial to them.

Preparing the content

  • Good content matters more than a good voice.
  • Write for the ear: compose word by word (manuscript method) but keep it spoken.
  • Short sentences that speak; suitable, simple words; avoid jargon, clichés, circumlocution, idioms and phrases for second-language audiences.
  • Use repetition and summarising (e.g. Martin Luther King’s ‘I have a dream’).
  • Insert transitions and signposts; plan places for pauses and shifts.

Visual aids

  • Use for technical subjects; pictures aid understanding.
  • Decide in advance where each aid will appear and mark it in the outline.
  • Make aids relevant, specific and accessible; avoid merely catchy ones.

Three parts: beginning, middle, end

  • Introduction – capture attention (quote, anecdote, story, e.g. Nehru’s ‘Tryst with destiny’); state purpose; preview main points; build credibility and rapport. About 2 minutes of a 20-minute talk; do not make it long.
  • Body – the heart of the matter; limit to two or three points, each explained and supported; simple, logical, stepwise.
  • Conclusion – inference from the body; restate and recapitulate so the audience carries something home; call for action in problem–solution talks; signal the end (‘to conclude…’); avoid an abrupt or limp ending.

Questions and notes

  • Encourage questions; anticipate them and prepare answers.
  • Prepare more material than required but be ready to skip.
  • Note cards with legible keywords; leave space in the script to look up at the audience.
  • Use quotations and references for support; create animation through word choice.

Summary

Plan an oral presentation by fixing the purpose, selecting a familiar yet fresh topic (using an inventory and brainstorming), analysing the audience and researching. Draft audience-specific content in short, simple, spoken sentences with repetition, signposts and planned visual aids. Structure it into a brief attention-grabbing introduction, a body of two or three well-supported points and a conclusion that recapitulates and, if needed, calls for action. Anticipate questions, prepare notes and extra material, and then practise.

Q6 Short What should the conclusion contain?
