Lecture 56: Drafting an Effective Resume

Week 12 · Course book PDF pages 925–947 (printed pages 924–946)

Learning objectives

  • Draft each section of an effective résumé: objective statement, education, additional qualifications, experience, activities and honours, special skills, references.
  • Apply action verbs and parallelism in résumé writing.
  • Explain what an application (covering) letter is and its three-paragraph structure.
  • Use specific, active, unbiased language in the cover letter.
  • Analyse a sample entry-level résumé.

Important definitions

Objective statement

A one-to-three-line summary of area of expertise and career interest, placed just after the heading; focused and specific.

Covering letter (application letter)

A short letter, much like a business or sales letter, whose main purpose is to attract the employer to read the résumé.

Parallelism

Using the same grammatical form for items in a list, e.g. each résumé bullet beginning with a past-tense action verb.

Professional references

Referees such as professors, project in-charge, heads or employers, as opposed to personal acquaintances.

Scannable letter

A letter in which each paragraph begins with a topic sentence so that it can be skimmed or scanned.

Action phrase

A phrase beginning with a strong verb, e.g. organised a debate, conducted a workshop, represented the college team.

Core concepts

Three parts of a résumé

Like every letter, a résumé has an opening, body and close. Remember: a résumé is only a passport; leave something for the interview. Demonstrate what you can do for the company, not what the company can do for you.

Objective statement

  • One to three lines, complete and focused; tells recruiters your skills.
  • Avoid generalised statements such as ‘a position allowing me to use my knowledge in different areas’.
  • Be specific. Examples: ‘a position allowing me to apply my background in engineering and high performance computing to biological problems’; ‘a marketing position with an opportunity for eventual managerial status requiring strong customer contact skills’.

Education

  • List in reverse order: latest first (master’s, then B.Tech); a graduate starts from graduation.
  • Give grade as on the marks sheet: CGPA if the institute uses CGPA, percentage otherwise.

Additional qualifications

  • Courses that make you stand out: German or French, programming languages, effective English, business law, ethics.
  • Never mention what you do not have.
  • Write the course title, not just a code like HS 001.

Employment experience

  • Freshers: internship (e.g. two months), practice school, project in an organisation, brief positions related to the job.
  • Use action verbs; replace weak words (got, help) by assisted, demonstrated, supervised, coordinated.
  • Examples: organised a debate during Cognizance 2017; conducted a workshop; represented the college team in volleyball.
  • Mention exact dates and place of internship; everything may be questioned in the interview.

Parallelism in bullets

Begin each bullet with the same form: oversaw the planning…, assisted in training and development, created a positive…, supervised, coordinated. Do not mix full sentences with fragments.

Activities and honours

  • Volunteering, representing the institute, awards, fellowships and visits abroad carry weight.
  • Include only those you can discuss at the interview.

Special skills

  • Two foreign languages (German, French) help in MNCs; NSS/social service shows the ‘extra mile’.
  • Name software specifically (C++, Oracle); give course duration or dates.

References

  • Preferably ‘available upon request’; if compulsory give professional references (professor, project in-charge, head, employer) with full address, phone and URL.
  • Inform the referee in advance, send your résumé, tell about the company.

Application (covering) letter

  • Like a business or sales letter; purpose: attract the employer to read the résumé.
  • Short; three paragraphs, no longer than one page.
  • Begin with a header giving the addressee’s name and address.
  • Paragraph 1: get attention (AIDA-like sales technique), mention the source of the advertisement and your suitability. E.g. ‘I believe that my knowledge of public relations and proven communication and leadership skills make me a strong candidate for the position of media relations coordinator…’.
  • Paragraph 2 (body): support candidacy with experience; show, do not tell; refer to the enclosed résumé. E.g. ‘As a banking representative at … I provided quality service while promoting sale of products…’ (must have a grain of truth).
  • Paragraph 3: emphatic close creating opportunity: ‘I would welcome the opportunity to discuss these and other qualifications with you’; give a working phone number; ask for a personal interview; end with thanks.

Language of the letter

  • Active voice; past tense for past work, present for present responsibilities; balance past and present.
  • Be specific with figures: ‘I initiated a program to attract customers to Pizza Hut which resulted in a 10 percent increase’ instead of ‘I attempted to attract customers’.
  • Avoid weak words (tried, helped, attempted), gender-biased words (use chairperson), jargon, clichés, redundancy.
  • Proofread; mail with résumé in a proper envelope; address company values (‘your esteemed organisation’).

Sample résumé (Nischay Buddhiraja)

  • Objective: ‘Position in sales or marketing with opportunity for advancement and travel.’
  • Academic profile with action verbs in past tense: scored 100 percentile, completed B.Tech, obtained, secured.
  • Achievements: presented a paper on the Aadhaar card and data security at IIT Delhi and won first prize; submitted a project on Electro Volt at Apogee, BITS Pilani.
  • Extracurricular activities; no referees because the format had no space (could write ‘on request’); personal details: date of birth, father’s name, languages, nationality, hobbies.

Summary

An effective résumé opens with a specific one-to-three-line objective, lists education from latest to earliest with grades as on the marks sheet, names real additional courses, uses parallel action-verb phrases for experience, highlights activities, honours and named special skills, and gives professional references only when needed. The covering letter, a short three-paragraph business/sales letter, attracts the reader in the first paragraph, shows rather than tells in the body, and closes emphatically with a request for an interview. Language should be active, specific, unbiased and error free.

Lecture 57: Facing Job Interviews: Part- I

Week 12 · Course book PDF pages 948–965 (printed pages 947–964)

Learning objectives

  • List the types of interview questions.
  • Explain how to be patient, positive and confident before and during an interview.
  • Describe the importance of appearance, personality, dress, grooming and bearing.
  • State the dress code for men and women.
  • Explain the role of voice, pauses, mannerisms and nonverbal cues.

Important definitions

Types of questions

Open, closed, probing, reflective, loaded, hypothetical and leading questions.

Tact

The skilful way of combining sentences and responding to questions without offending.

Personality test

An interview stage that judges personality traits and communication beyond subject knowledge.

Bearing

The manner of carrying oneself: facial expression, posture, enthusiasm.

Mannerisms

Habitual ways of behaving: how one looks, bends, turns, greets and responds.

Circumlocution

Roundabout questioning by interviewers that may puzzle the candidate.

Decorum

Appropriate, dignified behaviour and dress for a formal occasion.

Core concepts

Facing the call

  • An interview call brings delight and fear.
  • Success depends on how well you are prepared, not on contacts.
  • Most interviews are ‘decided in our brains’ by anticipation; tell yourself you can answer.

Types of questions

Open, closed, probing, reflective, loaded, hypothetical and leading questions (discussed in later lectures).

Patience, positivity, tact

  • Panel members may ask in quick succession; the best approach is patience.
  • Be confident and positive; be aware of likely questions; use tact and intuition; stay energetic.
  • Control your nerves; remain committed; do not show frustration by gesture or posture, since everything within is written on the face.

Conduct during questioning

  • Half of the battle is won by nonverbal behaviour.
  • Let the interviewer lead; do not answer before the question is complete.
  • Pause, stay composed, do not hurry or speak too fast; do not give the impression you know everything.
  • Listen throughout; answer as if responding, not imposing.

Bolstering confidence through appearance

  • Appearance covers dress, sitting posture, facial emotions, enthusiasm and preparations.
  • Appearances can be deceptive and experienced interviewers can detect it; do not beguile; present yourself as you are but positively. Quoted: ‘A false face must have a false heart within’ (Shakespeare, Macbeth).

Vocal characteristics and mannerisms

  • Neither too loud nor too fast; balance speed, rate, pitch.
  • Mannerisms (how you look at, bend towards or turn from different interviewers) can offend; stay composed.

Personality counts

  • Many selections include a personality test after the written test.
  • Dale Carnegie: personality is one of the important attributes for success; a New York department-store employment manager would rather hire a salesgirl who had not finished grade school but had a lovely smile than a PhD with a sober smile.
  • Employers prefer better communicators with good traits over degrees alone.

Dress and grooming (general)

  • Dress to suit the formality of the occasion: not gaudy, flamboyant or too loose; need not be expensive but should be simple, pleasant and clean.
  • Richard Bolles, What Color Is My Parachute?, is cited on dress and career choice.
  • Hair: dignified, not trendy or imitating celebrities (‘until you become a celebrity, be your own self’); short hair fares better; no long sideburns.
  • Face: warm, sincere; clean-shaven preferred, or neatly trimmed; trimmed nails.
  • Minimal cologne; minimal or no jewellery (no chains, bands, necklaces, earrings for men; rings kept minimum).

Dress for men

  • Shirt: good quality, button-down or classic colour, properly washed and ironed.
  • Suit: navy blue, dark brown or grey; natural-fibre blend; properly tailored, not designer; fresh and clean; two-piece.
  • Tie: good-quality blend, conservative stripes, simple pattern; tip should end near the centre of the belt.
  • Shoes: black (or brown), polished, not pointed or designer; dark socks.
  • Black or blue, not red; carry a small briefcase or portfolio.

Dress for women

  • Conservative business suit or full dress in natural woven blend and soft colour matching skin and hair.
  • Sensible polished pumps, not high heels.
  • Small purse; minimal nail polish; minimal jewellery: one set of earrings and no more than one ring on each hand.

Voice and pronunciation

  • Voice is the vehicle of expression: audible, clear, distinct, neither too fast nor too slow.
  • Use standard, understandable pronunciation, articulated clearly.
  • Use silence and pauses; interviewers are observing, not trying to eliminate you.

Tough questioning and mindset

  • Interviewers may rotate or twist questions; stay polite, flexible and tactful.
  • Each interview, even a failure, has meaning; the door is never closed. ‘You can fare well only when you can dare well.’

Summary

Lecture 58: Facing Job Interviews: Part II

Week 12 · Course book PDF pages 966–989 (printed pages 965–988)

Learning objectives

  • Define and exemplify the seven question types: open, closed, probing, reflective, loaded, hypothetical, leading.
  • Answer common interview questions about self, experience, strengths, weaknesses, money and ethics.
  • Handle inappropriate personal questions tactfully.
  • List what to do before, during and after the interview.
  • Recall Charles Dickens’ closing quotation.

Important definitions

Open question

A question inviting a free, varied, elaborate answer, usually asked at the start to make the candidate comfortable (e.g. Tell us briefly about yourself).

Closed question

A question with limited answers such as yes/no or a date (e.g. When did you complete your B.Tech?); often followed by a follow-up needing justification.

Probing question

A question that tests the candidate’s opinion, bias and attitude (e.g. Should students be allowed to take part in politics, including state and central politics?).

Reflective question

A question that mirrors the candidate’s statement or view to test rationale, logic and forward thinking (e.g. Am I right in thinking you are against opening many IITs and IIMs?).

Loaded question

A question in which the interviewer’s own bias or assumption is built in (e.g. Your GPA after first semester has been too low; how do you justify that?).

Hypothetical question

A question that places the candidate in an imagined unforeseen situation to test decision making (e.g. What would you do if disgruntled employees wanted to damage company property?).

Leading question

A question that suggests the answer the interviewer expects (e.g. Don’t you think our company is the best in reputation?).

Teetotaler

A person who does not drink alcohol.

Core concepts

Why know question types

  • Interviews include general questions, not only subject questions: integrity, honesty, sincerity, resourcefulness, initiative, leadership, communication.
  • The interview is a discourse in which meaning is negotiated; a wrong hire hurts the organisation for years.
  • Boards include subject experts and psychologists; how you answer matters as much as what.
  • Interviews last about 10–20 minutes.

Open questions

  • Asked at the beginning to create a comfortable atmosphere.
  • Examples: Tell us briefly about yourself; What is your area of expertise? How did you come to know about this job? What prompted you to apply? Why do you consider yourself suitable?
  • Answering is an art: indicate your qualities and experience briefly.

Closed questions

  • Answer limited to yes/no or exact facts: When did you complete graduation? Where did you receive first professional training? Do you know data processing? Do you have an advanced degree in electronics?
  • A follow-up such as ‘What is the relevance of electronics today?’ requires justification.

Probing questions

  • Test opinion, bias, attitude: ‘I believe students should be allowed to take part in politics. Should this include state and central politics?’
  • Give a reasoned opinion (e.g. politics develops leadership traits).

Reflective questions

  • Test rationale and forward thinking: ‘Am I rightly thinking you are against opening many IITs and IIMs?’; ‘So you want public sector companies to be totally privatised?’
  • Answers may differ (like the blind men and the elephant) but must be justified.

Loaded questions

  • The interviewer’s bias is embedded: ‘Your GPA is too low—how do you justify that?’; ‘Should places of religious disputes be converted into public parks?’
  • Responsibility for the reaction lies with the candidate; respond logically and calmly.

Hypothetical questions

  • Test decisions in unforeseen crises: ‘What if disgruntled employees want to damage company property?’; ‘How would you handle employees misusing company assets?’
  • Answer: first dialogue to understand the problem, then take action if needed; prevent others from being instigated.

Leading questions

  • Suggest the expected answer: ‘Don’t you think our company is the best in reputation?’; ‘Should the company be free to retrench people in a market tumble?’
  • Agree tactfully where harmless; where ethics is involved, respond ethically (e.g. call a meeting, share the burden, avoid retrenchment).

Experience and accomplishment questions

  • Why should we hire you when others have more experience? – state your qualities and fit.
  • You change jobs frequently? – give a positive, non-blaming reason; do not criticise a boss.
  • Team or alone? – prefer teams: save time, build relationships, better solutions.
  • Where do you see yourself after 5 years? – be modest; do not say ‘in your position’.
  • Boss dissatisfied with your work? – modest, receptive.
  • Foreign assignment? – be affirmative.
  • Co-worker supplying false data? – verify quietly from various sources.

Strengths, weaknesses, personal and money questions

  • Strength: state it affirmatively. Weakness: present it so it becomes a merit (e.g. perfectionism).
  • Alcohol: be honest (‘I am a teetotaler’ or ‘occasionally, not habituated’).
  • What if you are not selected? – hopeful, affirmative; learn from mistakes for the next interview.
  • Salary: do not disclose; refer to the advertised structure and your qualification.
  • Importance of money? – important, but not the only need; belonging matters.
  • Also hobbies, extracurriculars, general awareness (new products, nuclear policy, demonetisation), ethics (misuse of assets).

Inappropriate personal questions

  • Examples: divorced/separated? own house? maiden name? religious preference?
  • Do not overreact; answer rationally and positively: ‘I believe in humanity’; ‘I have lived in organisation accommodation and will own a house in due course’.

Before the interview

  • Select a comfortable, sober, clean, ironed dress; do not buy a new outfit obsessively.
  • Polished formal shoes; testimonials in order.
  • Read newspapers weeks ahead; anticipate current and general topics.

On the day

  • Reach before time; read the morning newspaper.
  • Talk to other candidates to ease nervousness; think positively and recall joyful memories.
  • Enter as if you belong; greet all; sit only when permitted; thank them.
  • Maintain eye contact with the questioner.
  • Do not accept an offer until all interviews are over, but never say no outright (e.g. lower position: ‘first let me get selected’).

After the interview

  • Do not turn back to look at the members; close the door gently; thank all and leave with a confident impression.
  • Quote: Charles Dickens – ‘Whatever I have tried to do in my life, I have tried to do well…I have always been thoroughly in earnest.’ Honesty is the best policy.
  • Interviews are continuous; ‘Prepare well, do well, perform well, and succeed well.’

Summary

Interviews use seven kinds of questions: open (comfort-building), closed (facts), probing (opinions), reflective (reasoning), loaded (embedded bias), hypothetical (crisis decisions) and leading (suggesting answers). Answers should be logical, honest, affirmative and justified: present weaknesses as merits, avoid disclosing salary or criticising employers, remain modest about the future, show team spirit and ethics, and handle personal questions calmly. Before the interview prepare dress, documents and current awareness; on the day arrive early, talk to other candidates, enter confidently, sit when asked, maintain eye contact and leave gracefully, closing the door gently.

Lecture 59: Emotional Intelligence and Critical Thinking

Week 12 · Course book PDF pages 990–1005 (printed pages 989–1004)

Learning objectives

  • Define emotional intelligence (EI) and critical thinking.
  • Differentiate EQ and IQ.
  • List the components of emotional intelligence: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills.
  • Distinguish personal and social competencies.
  • List the components of critical thinking and the social skills of influence, conflict management, leadership, change catalyst, building bonds, collaboration.

Important definitions

Emotional intelligence (EI)

The capacity for recognising our own feelings and those of others, for motivating ourselves and for managing emotions well in ourselves and in our relationships (popularised by Daniel Goleman in Working with Emotional Intelligence; the lecture names him ‘Richard Goleman’).

IQ (intelligence quotient)

A measure of mental ability or intelligence, related to academic abilities.

EQ (emotional quotient)

A measure of emotional intelligence: awareness and management of emotions in oneself and others.

Critical thinking

A mode of thinking about any content or subject in which the thinker improves the quality of thinking by skilfully taking charge of the structures inherent in thinking and imposing intellectual standards upon them.

Self-awareness

Awareness of one’s emotions, accurate self-assessment and self-confidence.

Self-regulation

Controlling emotions; includes trustworthiness, conscientiousness, adaptability and innovation.

Empathy

Understanding others’ emotions and putting oneself in their position.

Social skills

Influence, communication, conflict management, leadership, change catalyst, building bonds, collaboration and team capabilities.

Change catalyst

A person who initiates and brings change in the organisation.

Achievement drive

A personal competency of striving to do something new and excellent.

Core concepts

Why emotions matter at work

  • Everyone has emotions; compromises and control are needed at the workplace.
  • If driven by emotions, tasks are not completed; progress stalls.
  • Intelligence alone may not give correct decisions; groups use meetings and consensus.
  • Example clip: two managers argue over a conference room (‘conference rooms go to the highest ranked manager’), anger outbursts spoil the meeting.

Emotional intelligence

  • Ability to recognise, motivate and manage feelings in self and others; popularised by Goleman.
  • Balance between IQ and EI is needed for success.
  • Observation cited: women on average are more aware of their emotions, show more empathy and are more adept interpersonally.

EQ versus IQ

  • IQ relates to academic ability and mental ability; helps in getting a job.
  • EQ relates to emotions and relationships; helps to succeed; a true leader relies more on EQ without ignoring IQ.
  • Self-analysis is the first step in EI.

Components of EI (personal competence)

  1. Self-awareness: emotional awareness, accurate self-assessment, self-confidence (a ‘can do’ attitude; preparation plus self-belief).
  2. Self-regulation: self-control, trustworthiness (words match actions), conscientiousness (right versus wrong, ethics), adaptability (flexibility), innovation. Emotions cannot be left at home; nerves are controlled by telling oneself everything is in order.
  3. Motivation: achievement drive, commitment, initiative, optimism, determination. Example: Swachh Bharat Abhiyan began with one person’s drive.

Social competence

  1. Empathy / social awareness: understand others’ emotions, develop others, service orientation, awareness of diversity (cultures, faiths) and organisational politics; ‘brotherly feeling’ not a ‘big brother’.
  2. Social skills: influence (fairness, no bias, smooth communication), communication (Descartes quoted: ‘Thanks to language man became man’), conflict management (listening), leadership (listen to grievances), change catalyst, building bonds, collaboration and cooperation, team capabilities.

Critical thinking components

  • Imagination: anticipate what may happen in a meeting; keen observation; creative listening.
  • Asking relevant questions at the right time; detect fallacies.
  • Not being judgmental; hurried judgment is frightening.
  • Test the validity of points before discarding irrelevant ones.
  • Learning from experience; learn, unlearn; self-assessment and self-learning.
  • Examine relationships between people as well as statements.
  • Neutrality is the hallmark of a leader; assimilate everyone’s views.

Quotations and closing messages

  • Shakespeare: ‘What a piece of work is man’ – immense potential (even travel to Mars).
  • Robert Frost: ‘Two roads diverged in a wood, and I took the one less traveled by’ – be extraordinary; the wood stands for uncertainty.
  • ‘Difference of opinion is good, but let there be deference for difference of opinion’; be different, not indifferent.

Lecture 60: Applied Grammar

Week 12 · Course book PDF pages 1006–1026 (printed pages 1005–1025)

Learning objectives

  • Apply rules of plural nouns, nouns ending in s, and always-plural nouns.
  • Use capitalisation of titles, designations, departments and organisations correctly.
  • Use apostrophes for possession correctly.
  • Apply subject–verb agreement rules (none of, either/or, neither/nor, each of, indefinite pronouns, a great deal of).
  • Use articles, question tags, prepositions and voice correctly; avoid commonly confused words.

Important definitions

Grammar

Rules of a language and notions of correctness in its use.

Concord (agreement)

The verb must agree with its subject in number and person.

Appositive

A noun phrase (such as a name) placed beside another noun to identify it, e.g. only one professor, Jonathan Reeves.

Apostrophe

A mark showing possession or relationship, e.g. women’s, girls’ hostel; not used in yours.

Indefinite pronoun

A pronoun without specific reference: anybody, nobody, each, either, every, everyone; takes a singular verb.

Question tag

A short question added to a statement to seek confirmation, e.g. people are happy, aren’t they?

Active voice

The subject performs the action; verb follows the subject; greater impact.

Passive voice

The subject receives the action; order changes; less impact.

Definite article

‘the’: used for specific things, rivers, mountains, great books, newspapers, and for classes (the rich, the wise).

Core concepts

Why applied grammar

  • Grammar means rules and notions of correctness.
  • Even good speakers err in writing because of overuse, advertisements and digital media.
  • The lecture sensitises learners to shaky areas; it does not teach all grammar.

Sample incorrect sentences

  • I congratulate you for your success (should be on).
  • My student is a SDO (should be an S.D.O).
  • Where is your particular? (particulars).
  • Diabetes kill people (kills).
  • The secretary and principal are on leave (concord/article issue depending on meaning).
  • The man who is clad in jeans are my neighbour (is).

Nouns always plural

  • police (the police are coming), clothes (Where are my clothes?).
  • Tools and instruments in pairs: binoculars, scissors, spectacles, glasses, scales.
  • Garments: breeches, pants, jeans, pyjamas, trousers.
  • Children is already plural (not ‘childrens’); old plural brethren.

Nouns ending in s that are singular

  • news (no news is good news), politics, mathematics, physics, astrophysics, diseases diabetes, mumps.
  • Politics is an interesting game; Diabetes kills people silently.

Capitalisation

  • Capitalise titles before names: Professor Mishra, Doctor, surname.
  • Government posts as titles: Prime Minister, President, Vice Chairman.
  • Designation after the name is in small letters: Mr Pratyush Pant, director of Anshul Enterprises.
  • Do not capitalise when a title is followed by an appositive: only one professor, Jonathan Reeves, objected.
  • When a title replaces a name in a sentence, small letters: the director and the deputy director; capital only in an address (The Director, IIT Roorkee).
  • Do not capitalise with possessive pronouns: my supervisor, your brother, his guide.
  • Names of departments, units, cells: Educational Technology Cell; Dell’s Quality Control Unit; the firm’s Personnel Department; Nokia’s Corporate Social Responsibility unit.

Apostrophe

  • Shows ownership/relationship: one’s own, one year’s time, two years’ time, women’s associations, girls’ hostel, judges’ verdict, students’ views.
  • Never in yours, hers, its, ours, theirs (‘yours faithfully’, not ‘your’s’).
  • Names ending in s: both James’ book and James’s book; Keats’ / Keats’s poetry are correct.

Subject–verb agreement

  • Verb agrees with its subject in number and person; trace the true subject in long sentences.
  • None of + plural: usually plural (none of these managers have submitted…); singular when meaning ‘not one’ (none of them has come).
  • Singular subjects joined by either…or / neither…nor: singular verb (either the works manager or the shift in-charge has to take the responsibility).
  • If subjects differ in number/person, the verb agrees with the nearest subject: either he or I am to blame; neither the doctor nor the nurses were seen.
  • Each of, either of, neither of: third-person singular (each of these policies has its own limitations).
  • A great deal of / a good deal of: verb agrees with the object of ‘of’ (a great deal of money has been spent).
  • Most of the students feel…: agrees with the noun after ‘of’.
  • Indefinite pronouns (anybody, nobody, each, either, every, everyone): singular verb (each of the employees has to follow norms; everyone was recommended).

Articles

  • Choose a/an by sound, not spelling: an S.D.O, an apple, a university professor, a superintendent.
  • The for specifics and for: rivers and mountains (the Ganges, the Yamuna), great books (The Mahabharata, The Ramayana, The Bible, The Geeta, The Vedas), newspapers (The Times of India, The Telegraph, The Hindu), classes (the rich, the wise; Dr Johnson: ‘Let me smile with the wise and feed with the rich’).

Question tags

  • Positive statement → negative tag: people are happy, aren’t they?
  • Negative statement → positive tag: you do not like me, do you?
  • Exception: I am teaching well, aren’t I?
  • Use the same auxiliary: you have not enjoyed this course, have you?

Prepositions

  • senior, junior, superior, inferior take to, not than (Mr Rathore is senior to me).
  • Since for a point of time (since 2007); for for a period (for four years; for the last three days).
  • Beside = by the side of; besides = in addition to.
  • absorbed in; Roorkee lies between Meerut and Haridwar (two objects); beware of; comply with; confident of.

Voice and transformation

  • Active: verb follows subject; stronger impact. Passive: changed order; less impact (use for politeness or unknown doer).
  • Examples: ‘You are requested to submit the report’ (passive) vs ‘Please submit your report’ (active); ‘We expect good news’ vs ‘Good news is expected’; ‘The streets are swept every day’.
  • Meaning transformations: ‘too…to’ is negative (the hall is too small to accommodate thirty guests = so small that it cannot); an interrogative can carry negative meaning (Can a leopard change its skin? = it cannot); comparison (The aeroplane flies faster than birds = birds do not fly as fast as aeroplanes).

Confusing words

  • affect (verb: influence) / effect (noun: result).
  • formal / formerly (earlier).
  • loose (not tight) / lose (fail to keep).
  • stationary (still) / stationery (paper and writing material).
  • later (afterwards) / latter (second of two).
  • compliment (praise) / complement (complete); complimentary copy (free).
  • desert / dessert (sweet).
  • elicit (draw out) / illicit (illegal).

Course conclusion

  • The course covered communication in many phases: telephone, oral, written; personality; barriers; letters, reports, data; interviews, group discussion; leadership; speeches; balancing emotion and intellect.
  • ‘All is well that ends well.’ Grammar is a sea; one is only a drop in it.
